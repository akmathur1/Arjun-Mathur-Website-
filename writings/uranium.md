Earlier this month, in Covert Township, Michigan, workers lowered fuel assemblies back into a reactor that was shut in 2022 and sold to be dismantled. The Energy Secretary toured Palisades this week and said it will be running within weeks, on up to $1.52 billion of federal loan guarantees. In Pennsylvania, Constellation (CEG) expects fuel on site by year-end for the 835-megawatt unit it has renamed Crane and everyone else still calls Three Mile Island, under a twenty-year contract to sell the output to Microsoft. No shuttered American reactor has ever come back. Two are, one of them for a software company.

Count the gigawatts the AI companies have signed for, then count the new ones. Meta's 6.6 gigawatts by 2035, announced in January, is more than 2.1 from Vistra (VST) plants that have run for decades, 1.2 from an Oklo campus pencilled for 2030 at the earliest, and TerraPower reactors due no sooner than 2032. Amazon's 1,920 megawatts from Talen's (TLN) Susquehanna comes out of a plant already on the grid. Every deal has the same shape: output from reactors that already hold fuel. A change of customer is not a change of demand.

Which explains the strangest chart in commodities. The long-term contract price, the one utilities actually pay, ended September at $96.50 a pound, a nominal record above the $95 of 2007. Spot sat near $90. Uranium equities are down over 20 percent in a year, and the Sprott trust that does nothing but hold the metal traded this month at an 11.6 percent discount to what it holds. The term price is set by people buying fuel for 2030; the equities by people who wanted the trade to pay in 2026 and have left. I side with the fuel buyers.

Supply sits in one place. Kazakhstan produced 25,839 tonnes of uranium in 2025, about 40 percent of the world, nearly all of it by pumping sulphuric acid into sandstone, 70 to 80 kilograms of acid per kilogram of metal. Kazatomprom (KAP) has cut its nominal 2026 capacity about 10 percent, and Rosatom's Uranium One owns half or more of several of its mines. Mines covered about 90 percent of reactor requirements in 2024; the rest came out of stockpiles, and stockpiles do not refill.

But the mine is not the chokepoint. The chokepoint is three neutrons. Natural uranium is about 0.7 percent U-235, a reactor wants 3 to 5, and the only route is to turn the metal into uranium hexafluoride gas and spin it. Fluorine has a single stable isotope, so the whole difference between a molecule carrying U-235 and one carrying U-238 is three neutrons in roughly 350 mass units, and a centrifuge exploits it a sliver at a time, in cascades thousands of machines long.

Rosatom owns about 29 million of the world's 68 million separative work units, 43 percent. American utilities bought 26 percent of their enrichment from Russia in 2025, under a 2024 law that bans the trade and whose waivers expire on January 1, 2028. Urenco adds 700,000 units in New Mexico by early 2027. Centrus (LEU), with a $900 million federal contract signed in July, expects no new commercial capacity before 2029.

Lay the dates in a row. Power contracts signed in 2024 through 2026. Restarts fuelled in 2026 and 2027. Russian waivers lapse at the start of 2028. Western centrifuge halls arrive from 2029. The first reactors that are new rather than renamed arrive between 2030 and 2035, and a thousand-megawatt unit then wants about 140,000 separative work units a year for life. None of that is priced into a share of Cameco (CCJ) trading on this quarter's spot. My bet would be Cameco and Centrus, held through the stretch where nothing appears to happen.

The fuel going into Palisades this month already exists. The fuel that matters does not, and neither do the centrifuges that will spin it.
