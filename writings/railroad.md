A gallon of diesel moves a ton of freight nearly 500 miles on an American railroad. Union Pacific (UNP) burned 1.034 gallons per thousand gross ton-miles last quarter, and the number falls a percent or two a year because the physics is on its side: a steel wheel on a steel rail deforms by microns, a rubber tyre on asphalt by centimetres, and rolling resistance is the energy you lose putting the deformation back. Rail moves about 40 percent of long-distance ton-miles for 1.8 percent of transport emissions. Nothing on land comes close, and no single company has ever run the whole distance.

Union Pacific would like to be the first. On July 29, 2025 it agreed to buy Norfolk Southern for one share plus $88.82 in cash, $320 a share at the time and about $85 billion of enterprise value, promising $2.75 billion a year in savings and more than 50,000 route miles across 43 states, Pacific to Atlantic, under one dispatcher. Shareholders of both companies approved it in November with more than 99 percent of votes. If the regulator says no, Union Pacific pays Norfolk Southern $2.5 billion and walks.

The regulator is the Surface Transportation Board, and the decade in American freight is being settled inside a calendar almost nobody reads. The Board rejected the first application as incomplete in January: the railroads had projected no market shares past closing and left out the part of the merger agreement that defines when Union Pacific may walk. The revised filing arrived April 30. The Board accepted it on May 28 and froze the case for more detail on captive shippers, diversion and gateways. It lifted the freeze on August 18 and set the clock: comments November 18, replies February 16, briefs May 28, a decision by August 28, 2027. In September it refused to throw the case out early, as BNSF, CSX and five shipper groups asked, saying only that a deal of first impression deserved a full record.

First impression is the phrase that matters. This is the first merger judged under rules the Board wrote in 2001 and has never used, which require a Class I combination to enhance competition, not preserve it. The opponents say a coast-to-coast carrier cannot pass that test because its whole point is to remove the interchange, and the interchange is where a shipper gets a second quote. Union Pacific's answer is that the real competitor is the truck, and that autonomous trucks with no driver to pay or rest will take share from a network that loses a day or two at every hand-off. Its chief executive told a television interviewer this month he was 99.99 percent sure of approval and needs no divestitures. The chemical, fertilizer and grain shippers who would be left with one railroad in many places are less sure, and they pay the rate.

I think the shippers are right about the mechanism and the railroads are right about the truck, and the Board must pick which of two true things it fears more. What unsettles me is who picks. Five seats, four members: a chairman named by the President in January 2025, a vice chairman, a Democrat whose term runs to 2030, and a rail consultant sworn in this June. The fifth seat has been empty since the previous chairman was removed in August 2025 with no cause stated, a removal the Supreme Court's June ruling on independent agencies made all but unreviewable. No one has been nominated to it. There is no veto above these four, no appeal except to a court that defers to them, no ballot with their names on it.

The outcome will arrive as a press release with a number like 26-23. The railroad it describes will last a hundred years. The four people who decide it, you could not name.
