A gallon of diesel moves a ton of freight nearly 500 miles on an American railroad. Union Pacific (UNP) burned 1.034 gallons per thousand gross ton-miles last quarter, and that number falls a percent or two every year because the physics is on its side. A steel wheel on a steel rail deforms by microns, a rubber tyre on asphalt by centimetres, and rolling resistance is the energy you lose putting the deformation back. Rail moves about 40 percent of long-distance ton-miles for 1.8 percent of transport emissions. Nothing on land comes close, and no single company has ever run the whole distance.

Union Pacific would like to be the first. On July 29, 2025 it agreed to buy Norfolk Southern for one share plus $88.82 in cash, $320 a share at the time and about $85 billion of enterprise value, promising $2.75 billion a year in savings and more than 50,000 route miles across 43 states, Pacific to Atlantic, under one dispatcher. Shareholders of both companies approved it in November with more than 99 percent of the votes. If the regulator says no, Union Pacific pays Norfolk Southern $2.5 billion and walks away.

The regulator is the Surface Transportation Board, and the next decade of American freight is being settled inside a calendar almost nobody reads. In January the Board rejected the first application as incomplete, because the railroads had projected no market shares past closing and had left out the part of the merger agreement that defines when Union Pacific may walk. A revised filing arrived April 30, the Board accepted it on May 28 and then froze the case to get more detail on captive shippers, diversion and gateways. It lifted the freeze on August 18 and set the clock: comments November 18, replies February 16, briefs May 28, a decision by August 28, 2027. In September it refused to throw the case out early, as BNSF, CSX and five shipper groups had asked, saying only that a deal of first impression deserved a full record.

First impression is the phrase to watch. This is the first merger to be judged under rules the Board wrote in 2001 and has never once used, which require a Class I combination to enhance competition rather than just preserve it. The opponents say a coast-to-coast carrier can't pass that test because its whole point is to remove the interchange, and the interchange is where a shipper gets a second quote. Union Pacific's answer is that the real competitor is the truck, and that autonomous trucks with no driver to pay or rest will take share from a network that loses a day or two at every hand-off. Its chief executive told a television interviewer in September he was 99.99 percent sure of approval and needs no divestitures. The chemical, fertilizer and grain shippers who would be left with one railroad in a lot of places are less sure, and they're the ones who pay the rate.

My own view is that the shippers are right about the mechanism and the railroads are right about the truck, and the Board has to pick which of two true things it fears more. What bothers me is who gets to pick. Five seats, four members: a chairman named by the President in January 2025, a vice chairman, a Democrat whose term runs to 2030, and a rail consultant sworn in this June. The fifth seat has been empty since the previous chairman was removed in August 2025 with no cause stated, a removal that the Supreme Court's June ruling on independent agencies made all but unreviewable. Nobody has been nominated to fill it. There's no veto above these four, no appeal except to a court that defers to them, and no ballot with their names on it.

The decision will arrive as a press release with a number on it like 26-23. The railroad it describes will last a hundred years. I couldn't have named the four people deciding it before I started reading the docket, and I'd guess you couldn't either.
