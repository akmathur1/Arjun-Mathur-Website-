On October 22 QatarEnergy signed to buy 4 million tonnes a year of liquefied natural gas from Cheniere (LNG), half from Sabine Pass and half from Corpus Christi, at Henry Hub plus a fixed fee, through 2054. A week later it signed for 3 million more from Woodside's (WDS) plant in Louisiana, twenty years, first cargoes in 2030. The country that shipped 82 million tonnes of LNG in 2025, a fifth of everything traded on earth, is now, by its own energy minister's count, the largest buyer of American LNG, and he says the final number will be "much higher."

The reason is a date. On February 28 the United States and Israel began striking Iran, and Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, which carried a fifth of the world's LNG. In early March Iranian drones hit Ras Laffan, QatarEnergy halted production and declared force majeure. Then a missile strike disabled two of the fourteen trains there, 12.8 million tonnes a year, 17 percent of Qatar's capacity, with a repair time of three to five years. There was a ceasefire in April, an agreement in June, a collapse in July, and a US blockade of Iranian ports reimposed on July 14 that is still in force. The force majeure now runs to year-end. The Dutch benchmark passed €97 a megawatt-hour at the end of October, its highest since 2022, and Asian spot is above $31 per million British thermal units against $2.60 at Henry Hub.

If you read the Cheniere contract against that spread, it isn't really a gas purchase. Qatar has 77 million tonnes of capacity and is building toward 142. It doesn't need Louisiana molecules. What it has bought is an option on the strait: twenty years of gas that loads on a coast nobody can close, priced off the Gulf Coast and resold at whatever price the Gulf sets. The minister more or less said this out loud, that pipelines around Hormuz "can be also targets" and that these deals need no capital and earn money the day the strait shuts. The tenant of the most dangerous waterway on earth has gone and bought insurance in Texas.

This is where I part ways with the people pricing a golden decade for American gas. The supply wave was real before the war and it's still coming: 234 million tonnes a year of liquefaction is under construction or approved worldwide, nearly half of it American, on top of 525 already operating. Venture Global (VG) closed $8.6 billion for the second phase of CP2 in March. Commonwealth and Delfin followed. The Energy Department has approved more than 18.6 billion cubic feet a day of exports since the January 2025 order ended the permitting pause, and first-half exports ran 23 percent above last year. Most of that gas is sold at Henry Hub plus a fee, meaning the seller gets paid for liquefaction and the buyer owns the spread.

So the question I keep asking is what the spread looks like in 2030, when the plants being financed at today's $31 actually arrive. The IEA says the war delays the easing effect of the supply wave by at least two years and removes about 15 percent of the new supply expected through 2030. That's a delay, not a cancellation. Qatar's undamaged trains restart within a week of a peace deal, the first North Field expansion train is slated for early 2027, and the buyers shopping now did the opposite when the price spiked: Pakistan's first-half imports fell 47 percent, India burned coal, China took 12 percent less for four months. Demand at $31 is demand that has already started to leave. A liquefaction train is a thirty-year asset being sanctioned in an eight-month price spike, and a fixed fee only looks fixed until the person paying it runs the numbers on a $9 Asian market in 2031.

The strait will reopen at some point. Every contract signed this month is a bet on when, and the party with the best information about the answer is the one that has started buying. I notice that, and I'm not sure the gas bulls do.
