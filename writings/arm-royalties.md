Count the Arm chips within reach of wherever you're sitting. A phone has one for the apps, another in the modem, another for Bluetooth, and the SIM card inside it is often an Arm core as well. Earbuds carry one each, and so do the watch, the laptop, the router, the chip on a bank card, and many of the controllers in a car. My count for a desk comes to between two and ten. In the quarter that ended in September, Arm's licensees reported shipping more than ten billion of them, a first, and the running total since 1990 passed 350 billion in the spring.

The company behind that number is in Cambridge and until this year had never sold a chip. Arm (ARM) began in November 1990 as twelve engineers from Acorn Computers in a converted turkey barn, with Apple and VLSI Technology as partners. Its first big product, the chip in Apple's Newton, flopped, and in 1993 Robin Saxby turned the firm into something odder than a chipmaker. Arm would design the instruction set and the cores, license them to anyone who asked, and take a fee up front plus a royalty on every chip that shipped. Thirty-six years later that's still the whole business. About 99 percent of smartphones run on it, Apple moved the Mac to it in 2020, and Arm says it now holds about half the CPU compute at the largest cloud companies.

The royalty is tiny and the base is enormous. Last quarter's $670 million of royalties on ten billion chips is under seven cents a chip. The fiscal year to March brought $2.61 billion of royalties and $2.31 billion of license fees from two kinds of customer. Most buy finished core designs. A handful, Apple and Qualcomm among them, hold architecture licenses that let them design their own cores, provided the cores run Arm's instruction set and pay the royalty anyway. The instruction set is the interface between every compiled program and the silicon, and interfaces are where the rent is: leave Arm and you recompile the world. That's why the second kind of customer resents it. The better your own engineers get, the more the seven cents looks like a tax on work you did yourself.

The sell side models Arm as an AI company. It closed Monday at 207 times trailing earnings and 70 times next year's, a $194 billion market value on $5.5 billion of revenue, with SoftBank still holding 87 percent. What I'd model instead is that in March Arm broke its own rule. It announced the AGI CPU, a 136-core data center processor it designed and sells as finished silicon, with Meta as the lead customer. For 33 years the deal was that Arm would never turn up on the other side of a sales call. Now it does. Bloomberg reported in May that the FTC is examining whether Arm would degrade the licenses it sells to companies it now competes with. In October a Delaware jury found that Arm had breached its contract with Qualcomm (QCOM) and interfered with a Qualcomm deal at Meta. Qualcomm asked for $1.5 billion and got two dollars, and the judge still has to decide whether Qualcomm can stop paying Arm royalties for five years.

License revenue grew 50 percent last quarter, as customers buy the newest cores for their own AI chips, and royalties grew 8 percent. What I'd want to know before paying 70 times next year's earnings is how many of those customers decide the royalty now funds a competitor and start leaving. RISC-V exists for that purpose, and the companies best placed to move are the ones Arm just sued or started selling against. The royalty was easy to pay for 35 years because Arm couldn't compete with the people paying it. Since March it can, and the probe and the verdict are the first invoices.
