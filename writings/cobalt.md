Every article about Congo's minerals contains the number $24 trillion, and I have yet to find one that shows the arithmetic, so I do not use it. Here are numbers somebody did count. In 2025 the Democratic Republic of Congo mined 230,000 of the world's 310,000 tonnes of cobalt, 73 percent, and holds 6 million of the 12 million tonnes of known reserves. Half the metal still in the ground sits in one country, and China refines about four fifths of what comes out.

On February 22, 2025, with cobalt below $10 a pound at a nine-year low, Kinshasa closed the border to it. Exports stopped for eight months. In October the ban became a quota: 96,600 tonnes a year for 2026 and 2027, 9,600 of them reserved for the state, from a country that had just mined 230,000. Exports were cut by more than half by decree, with no mine closing and no ore running out: the purest price-setting experiment any commodity has run this decade.

It worked, for a year. Cobalt averaged $21 a pound in America in 2025 against $16.77 in 2024; hydroxide out of Congo roughly quadrupled from where it stood before the ban; by late April 2026 metal was assessed near $56,000 a tonne. Then the rationer met the variable it does not control. By August the agency that sets the quotas was saying it might cut them further "to help restore market balance," which is what a cartel says when the price is falling. On October 28 cobalt was about $34,600 a tonne, down 29 percent in a year and 12 percent in a month.

The miners told you who they are by how they responded. CMOC (3993.HK), Chinese, runs Tenke Fungurume and Kisanfu and produced 117,549 tonnes last year, more than half of Congo's output and about 38 percent of the world's, from two mines. It kept its 2026 guidance at 100,000 to 120,000 tonnes against an estimated export quota near 31,000, so most of what it digs goes into a stockpile to wait. Glencore (GLEN) went the other way: its cobalt output fell 46 percent in the first half of 2026, Mutanda produced none, and the company says its Congolese assets now prioritise copper, with the cobalt held in solution to be sold "as export regulations evolve." Copper is at a record. Cobalt is the thing you leave in the tank. Canada, where it comes up as a byproduct of nickel, mined 3,500 tonnes in 2025, eleven days of CMOC. Indonesia mined 44,000, 14 percent of the world, as a byproduct of its nickel acid-leach plants, and no quota in Kinshasa reaches it.

Congo has rationed a metal the battery industry has spent a decade designing out. Cobalt's job in a cathode is structural: it keeps the layered nickel-manganese oxide lattice ordered while lithium moves in and out, and stops nickel wandering into lithium's sites. Lithium iron phosphate uses a phosphate framework rigid enough to need no cobalt at all, and it took 51 percent of the global battery market in 2024 and 79 percent of that year's growth. Cobalt-bearing chemistries fell from 60 to 53 percent of EV battery demand in one year. Within the chemistries that still use it, moving from an equal nickel-manganese-cobalt mix to the 8:1:1 formula cuts the cobalt per kilowatt-hour by more than half. An EV carries five to ten kilograms. The number is falling in both directions, by share and by dose.

OPEC rations a molecule nobody has replaced. Kinshasa is rationing one that already has a replacement with a lower bill of materials and a better safety record, and every month the price sits above the cost of switching, more cathode lines switch. The quota does not create demand. It brings forward the day the demand leaves.

The quota runs to the end of 2027, which is longer than the chemistry will wait.
