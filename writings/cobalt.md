Every article about Congo's minerals has the number $24 trillion in it somewhere, and I have yet to find one that shows where it comes from, so I'm not going to use it. Here are some numbers somebody actually counted. In 2025 the Democratic Republic of Congo mined 230,000 of the world's 310,000 tonnes of cobalt, which is 73 percent, and it holds 6 million of the 12 million tonnes of known reserves. Half the metal still in the ground is in one country, and China refines about four fifths of what comes out of it.

On February 22, 2025, with cobalt under $10 a pound at a nine-year low, Kinshasa closed the border to it. Exports stopped for eight months. In October the ban turned into a quota: 96,600 tonnes a year for 2026 and 2027, 9,600 of them reserved for the state, from a country that had just mined 230,000. So exports were cut by more than half by decree, without a mine closing or an ore body running out. I can't think of a purer price-setting experiment any commodity has run this decade.

For about a year it worked. Cobalt averaged $21 a pound in the US in 2025 against $16.77 in 2024. Hydroxide out of Congo roughly quadrupled from where it sat before the ban, and by late April 2026 metal was being assessed near $56,000 a tonne. Then the quota ran into the one variable it doesn't control. By August the agency that sets it was saying it might cut the quota further "to help restore market balance," which is the phrase a cartel uses when the price is falling. On October 28 cobalt was about $34,600 a tonne, down 29 percent in a year and 12 percent in a month.

You can tell what the miners think from how they responded. CMOC (3993.HK), which is Chinese, runs Tenke Fungurume and Kisanfu and produced 117,549 tonnes last year, more than half of Congo's output and about 38 percent of the world's, out of two mines. It kept its 2026 guidance at 100,000 to 120,000 tonnes against an estimated export quota of around 31,000, so most of what it digs is going into a stockpile to wait. Glencore (GLEN) went the other way. Its cobalt output fell 46 percent in the first half of 2026, Mutanda produced none, and the company now says its Congolese assets prioritize copper, with the cobalt held in solution to be sold "as export regulations evolve." Copper is at a record, so that's an easy call. Cobalt is the thing you leave in the tank. For scale, Canada, where cobalt comes up as a byproduct of nickel, mined 3,500 tonnes in 2025, which is about eleven days of CMOC. Indonesia mined 44,000, 14 percent of the world, as a byproduct of its nickel acid-leach plants, and no quota written in Kinshasa reaches it.

The bigger problem for Congo is that it's rationing a metal the battery industry has spent a decade designing out. Cobalt's job in a cathode is structural. It keeps the layered nickel-manganese oxide lattice ordered while lithium moves in and out, and stops nickel wandering into lithium's sites. Lithium iron phosphate uses a phosphate framework that's rigid enough to need no cobalt at all, and LFP took 51 percent of the global battery market in 2024 and 79 percent of that year's growth. Cobalt-bearing chemistries fell from 60 to 53 percent of EV battery demand in a single year. Even inside the chemistries that still use it, going from an equal nickel-manganese-cobalt mix to the 8:1:1 formula cuts the cobalt per kilowatt-hour by more than half. An EV carries five to ten kilograms. The number is falling by share and by dose at the same time.

OPEC rations a molecule nobody has replaced. Kinshasa is rationing one that already has a replacement with a lower bill of materials and a better safety record, and every month the price sits above the cost of switching, a few more cathode lines switch. I don't think the quota creates any demand. If anything it brings forward the day the demand leaves. It runs to the end of 2027, and I doubt the chemistry waits that long.
