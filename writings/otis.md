There are about 23 million elevators and escalators in service on earth, and the count grows at a mid-single-digit pace because every new building needs one and almost no old building gives one back. Otis (OTIS) maintains about 2.5 million of them, roughly one in nine. That is the largest service book in the industry and a smaller share than the company's fame suggests; I have heard "a fifth of the world's elevators" from people who should know better. It moves 2.5 billion people a day with 72,000 employees, 45,000 of them in the field with a toolbag.

The share is not the number that matters. The split is. Last year Otis sold $5 billion of new equipment and earned $240 million on it, a margin under 5 percent. It sold $9.4 billion of service, maintenance, repair and modernization, and earned $2.37 billion, a record 25.1 percent margin. Service was 65 percent of sales and 91 percent of segment profit. The new elevator is the razor, sold near cost to get into the shaft. The service contract is the blade, sold every month for as long as the building stands, and a building changes owners, tenants and names far more often than it changes elevator contractors.

In my own field this is the difference between nucleation and growth. Forming the first droplet of a new phase costs free energy; the barrier is high and most attempts dissolve. Once a droplet exists above its critical size, growth is downhill and nearly free. Installation is the nucleation event, and Otis pays for it at a 5 percent margin. Everything after is growth.

The trouble is that nucleation has slowed where it used to happen most. Global new-equipment units fell 6 percent last year to 735,000, driven by a 13 percent drop in China, and Otis's own Chinese new-equipment sales and orders both fell by high-teens percentages last quarter. China is about a fifth of its new-equipment sales and 11 percent of total sales. In July the company cut its 2026 earnings guidance to $4.01 to $4.05 a share from $4.20 to $4.24. The stock is down 18 percent in twelve months to about $75, 19 times earnings, a 2.3 percent dividend, a $28.6 billion company. Deutsche Bank has it as a short-term sell, Wells Fargo started it at underweight, and the chief executive retires next summer.

Here is what the sellers have right. A Chinese apartment boom that is not coming back means fewer razors installed in China for years, and every unit not installed in 2026 is a service contract not signed in 2028 and not renewed in 2040. The damage is real, and it is slow, which is the worst kind for a stock because it never produces the single bad quarter that clears the air.

Here is what they have wrong. They are pricing a company that earns 91 percent of its profit from service on its 9 percent new-equipment problem. The maintenance portfolio grew 4 percent for the fourth consecutive year. Modernization, the business of ripping out a 1990s controller and keeping the shaft, grew 24 percent last quarter with a backlog up 24 percent, because an installed base ages whether or not anyone is building. The 23 million units already installed are the market. The 735,000 new ones a year are the marketing.

Otis was spun out of United Technologies on April 3, 2020, three weeks into a pandemic, and has spent six years explaining to a market that wanted a machinery stock that it is a subscription business with a factory attached. I would rather own the blades than the razor, and at 19 times earnings I would rather own them a little cheaper than this. But nobody has ever moved into a building and asked who made the elevator. Everybody knows who they call when it stops.
