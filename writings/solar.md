In May 2025 China connected 93 gigawatts of solar to its grid in a single month. The modules had fallen below ten cents a watt at the factory gate the year before, a price that buys the tempered glass, the aluminum frame, the encapsulant, the junction box and the sliced silicon that turns photons into current, and the factories were losing money at it. Prices are down more than 60 percent since 2023, margins at the largest producers are around minus ten percent, and the industry has racked up close to $5 billion of cumulative losses since the start of 2024. The cheapest electricity-generating object ever built is being sold below cost by an industry that can't stop.

Every stage upstream is Chinese too: about 92 percent of the world's polysilicon, 98 percent of its wafers, more than 90 percent of its cells, four fifths of its modules, and China is the main maker of the glass and the encapsulant film besides. The IEA expects concentration in the key segments to stay above 90 percent through 2030. First Solar (FSLR) is the one real exception, and it got there by leaving silicon out: a cadmium telluride film a few microns thick deposited straight onto glass in Ohio, which makes it the one large module maker a tariff on Chinese silicon can't touch.

So the panel is solved. Why is solar still hard here? My answer is that the panel is the only part that got cheap, and everything else is priced in American hours and American permits. A rooftop job is a crew, an inverter, racking, an electrician, a permit, a utility inspection and a salesman, and the panel is now a minor line on that invoice. GAF Energy's solar shingles and Tesla's solar roof are both attacks on the labor side of the same problem: if the roofer is already up there, let the roof be the panel. Utility scale is worse. The IEA's own words for the United States are grid queue backlogs and interconnection delays, and a project stuck in a queue pays interest on land it can't energize.

One distinction I wish more people kept straight: a megawatt is a rate and a megawatt-hour is an amount. A 100-megawatt farm makes 100 megawatt-hours in a bright hour and nothing at midnight, and the grid gets paid for the second number while being built for the first.

In a chain of reactions the overall rate is set by the slowest step, and speeding up the fast ones changes nothing. China spent a decade making the panel step effectively instantaneous. The slow step in America is a transformer order, a county permit and a utility engineer with a queue, and Washington has lately made it slower. The residential tax credit died at the end of 2025. Utility-scale projects have to be in service by the end of 2027 unless construction began within a year of the new law. Foreign-entity rules now reach into the Chinese supply chain, which is the only supply chain there is, and Chinese cells and modules carry a 50 percent Section 301 tariff on top. The IEA cut its forecast for American solar by almost 40 percent, 140 gigawatts less by 2030, and for residential by almost 70. Constellation (CEG), the country's largest nuclear operator, is the opposite bet and somehow the same bet: firm power from plants that are already connected, where the connection is the asset.

People keep telling me solar quadruples by 2030. It doesn't, at least not in any forecast I can find. The IEA's main case has the world's renewable capacity a little more than doubling, 4,600 gigawatts added between 2025 and 2030 with solar nearly 80 percent of it, and solar's share of the world's electricity going from 8 percent to 15. So call it a doubling, carried by a country with a desert and a grid it orders rather than queues for. China is nearly 60 percent of the growth and will have solar above a fifth of its own generation by 2030.

The sun is free everywhere. The paperwork, it turns out, is priced locally, and that's the number I'd watch.
