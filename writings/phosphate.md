A conveyor belt about a hundred kilometres long runs from the Bou Craa mine, in the part of Western Sahara that Morocco administers and the International Court of Justice has declined to call Moroccan, down to the Atlantic. It is the longest conveyor in the world, and the dust blowing off it is visible from orbit. Last year 2.02 million tonnes of rock went down that belt onto 36 ships for exactly three buyers, in India, Mexico and New Zealand. Everyone else has dropped away; the European Court of Justice ruled again in 2024 that the Saharawi have to consent, and they have not.

Bou Craa is about eight percent of what OCP digs. The number that matters sits in the Geological Survey's table: of the world's 73 billion tonnes of phosphate rock reserves, Morocco holds 50 billion, about 70 percent. China holds 3.4 billion, the United States one. Beneath the table, in the flat prose the Survey uses for every commodity: there are no substitutes for phosphorus in agriculture. There are none anywhere. The backbone of DNA alternates sugar and phosphate; the molecule every cell spends as energy is a triphosphate; bone is calcium phosphate. In my own corner of science, the way a cell tells a protein condensate to form or dissolve is to bolt a phosphate group onto the protein. Nitrogen you can pull from the air with enough natural gas. Phosphorus you dig.

Here is where the reserve number misleads. Reserves are about the future; production is about the price. China mined 110 million of the world's 250 million tonnes in 2025, Morocco 36, the United States 20. Divide the ground by the digging and China empties its reserve in about thirty years, America in fifty, Morocco in fourteen centuries. And this year's price shock had almost nothing to do with rock. A tonne of diammonium phosphate takes 1.5 to 2 tonnes of rock, 0.4 of sulfur and 0.2 of ammonia, and roughly 44 percent of seaborne sulfur loads in the Gulf. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively shut for most of the eight months since the American and Israeli strikes of February 28; transits run in single digits a day against 85 before. Sulfur went from under $100 a ton in 2024 to above $1,000 by late summer. China, which exported 10 million tons of DAP and MAP in 2021, closed its exports in mid-March. Illinois DAP was $929 a ton on October 16, against $600 to $700 through 2024 and the first half of 2025. Morocco holds the rock. The sulfur comes from somewhere else, and this year somewhere else was on fire.

Washington noticed late. Since April 2021 the United States had charged a countervailing duty of more than 16 percent on Moroccan phosphate fertilizer, at the petition of Mosaic (MOS), which runs the Florida mines; Texas A&M puts the cost to farmers at $6.9 billion over five seasons. On June 29 the president declared an emergency and suspended the duty for eight months, and in August the first Moroccan cargo in five years, 54,000 tonnes of triple superphosphate, docked at New Orleans. The same summer Mosaic curtailed production in Florida and Louisiana for want of sulfur. The tariff protected a producer that cannot make the product without the Gulf, against a producer that owns the ground.

Which brings me to the strange part. OCP is 94 percent owned by the Moroccan state, the rest by a bank's funds, a pension fund and a university endowment; a 2008 law gives it the sole right to mine phosphate in the kingdom; it took in about $11.4 billion last year, up 17 percent. Its dollar bonds trade in Dublin. Its shares trade nowhere. You can lend to the most consequential monopoly in food. You cannot own it.

The last phosphate mine on earth will be Moroccan, and it will not have a ticker.
