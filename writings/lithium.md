In the middle of May a tonne of battery-grade lithium carbonate traded above 200,000 yuan on the Guangzhou futures exchange, a two-year high. On October 22 the main contract closed at 94,180. In dollars, Fastmarkets' seaborne assessment went from $24.50 to $25.80 a kilogram on May 11 to $12.80 to $15.00 on October 27. Nothing happened to the metal in between. Zimbabwe halted concentrate exports in February, a CATL mine in Jiangxi stayed shut, and the market decided there was a shortage. Then two Australian mines restarted, China's main price reporter restated national inventory upward by roughly 100,000 tonnes in a single September morning, and a bearish Goldman note on October 12 helped take the contract down 11.8 percent in a day.

If I were going to own lithium at all I'd own the people who dig it rather than the futures, and the last five months are a decent illustration of why neither has been comfortable. The chemistry is what keeps pulling me back to this, so let me at least get it right. Lithium is element three, the lightest metal there is, and its standard reduction potential is about minus 3.04 volts, the most negative on the table, which is why it sits at the anode. A cell built on lithium delivers more voltage than one built on anything else. Energy is charge times voltage, and lithium supplies both terms, the most volts per cell and, because each atom weighs almost nothing, the most charge per gram. That's the entire bull case, and it's true.

What the bull case leaves out is the supply response. In 2017 the world consumed about 41,500 tonnes of lithium and battery-grade carbonate averaged $13,900 a tonne. In 2025 the world consumed about 263,000 tonnes, more than six times as much, and the American annual average price was $9,000. Batteries went from 46 percent of demand to 88. So demand did exactly what the decks promised, and the price, after a visit to $63,700 in 2022, came all the way back down, because lithium isn't scarce. It's dissolved in salt flats and locked in hard rock across Australia, Zimbabwe, Brazil, Mali and Argentina, and every one of those places answered the 2022 price within three years. Scarcity turned out to be a story about rocks, and price a story about how fast people can dig, and people were quick.

The geography is the part I find beautiful, for what it's worth. Under the salt crust of the Salar de Atacama in northern Chile the brine gets pumped up into ponds, and the desert sun does the first stage of refining for free. That salar, Chile's corner of the lithium triangle it shares with Argentina and Bolivia, gave the world 56,000 tonnes last year. That's a fifth of mine supply, not the 30 percent people still quote (I quoted it myself until I checked the USGS table). Australia's hard rock gave 92,000 and China 62,000. SQM (SQM), which pumps the Atacama alongside Albemarle (ALB), folded its operation last December into a joint venture with the state copper company that runs to 2060, which is Chile's way of saying it has noticed what the brine is worth.

The majors have voted with cash. Rio Tinto (RIO) paid $6.7 billion for Arcadium in March 2025, buying Argentine brine near the bottom of the cycle. Standard Lithium (SLI) and Equinor are a final investment decision away from pulling 22,500 tonnes a year of carbonate out of the Smackover brine under southwest Arkansas, with $225 million from the Department of Energy attached and first production pencilled in for 2028. Until then the United States has one producing operation, Albemarle's ponds at Silver Peak, Nevada, so small that the Geological Survey withholds its output to protect the company. A country paying for a battery supply chain mines almost none of the lithium that goes in it.

One last thing I like about this element. Lithium carbonate was a psychiatric drug long before it was a battery. An Australian doctor started giving it to manic patients in 1949, and medicine is still about one percent of demand. It has been steadying minds for seventy-seven years. It has never once steadied a price.
