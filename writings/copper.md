On September 8, 2025, about 800,000 tonnes of wet mud moved through the Grasberg Block Cave in Papua and killed seven workers. Freeport-McMoRan (FCX) declared force majeure on the 24th, cut its 2026 guidance for what is the world's second-largest copper mine by roughly 35 percent, and still isn't back at full rates. That was the third big blow of the year. In May a seismic event flooded the Kakula mine in Congo and Ivanhoe (IVN) withdrew its guidance; its 2026 target is now 380,000 to 420,000 tonnes from a complex that was meant to do 550,000. On July 31 a collapse at El Teniente in Chile killed six workers, Codelco suspended the mine's deep expansion in August over seismic risk, and an avalanche there killed two more on October 11. Escondida, the largest copper mine on earth, shut after a fatal accident on September 23.

Benchmark put the Grasberg loss alone at 591,000 tonnes through the end of 2026, about 2.6 percent of a 23-million-tonne world. Global mine output fell 1 percent in the first seven months of the year and Chilean output fell 7 percent. Smelters are so short of concentrate that they're paying miners $272 a tonne to take it, when in a normal year the money flows the other way. Copper started 2026 at $12,423 a tonne, printed $14,875 on September 10, settled at a record $14,822 for cash on October 12, and was sitting near $14,500 in late October, up about a third on a year earlier.

People tell me South America controls this market, and that's true until you count. Chile mined 5.3 million tonnes in 2025 and Peru 2.7 million, 35 percent of the world between them, and Congo at 3.2 million now out-produces Peru. What Chile actually has is a toll booth. Codelco, the state miner, digs about a quarter of the country's copper, booked 26,875 tonnes in 2025 that an internal audit later found it hadn't produced, and mined 11 percent less in the first half of 2026. The royalty law in force since January 2024 takes the effective tax on large miners to about 45 percent. Mining is 57 percent of Chile's exports. A country that dependent on one metal doesn't really control the price. It just hopes the price holds.

The question I started with was what the shortage did to renewables, and the answer is less than I expected, for arithmetic reasons. An electric car carries about 53 kilograms of copper against 22 in a petrol car. At $14.50 a kilogram that's $770 of copper in the EV, and the whole rally of the past year added about $190 to it. Offshore wind uses about eight tonnes per megawatt against roughly one for a coal plant, so a megawatt of turbine carries $116,000 of copper, which is a rounding error on hardware that costs millions per megawatt installed. So the transition didn't get expensive because copper did. Copper got expensive because the transition is real, because a data center needs 20 to 40 tonnes per megawatt, and because China's grid spending rose 37 percent in the first quarter.

That's the thing neither the climate deck nor the mining deck likes to say out loud. Solar, wind, gas, nuclear and the server hall disagree about everything except the conductor. Whatever makes the electron, copper carries it, with a resistivity only silver beats, and aluminum, the one real substitute, needs about 60 percent more cross-section to do the same job. The energy transition is, to a large degree, a copper trade with a climate label on it, and the label is what gets the subsidy.

One caveat on the record price. More than 700,000 tonnes are sitting in US warehouses, pulled in ahead of a Section 232 tariff decision on refined copper that has been pending since June, and Macquarie expects it to still be pending at New Year. COMEX stocks are up roughly 700 percent since February 2025 and forecasters still pencil in a small surplus for 2026. Some of the all-time high is metal hiding in American sheds.

Three accidents moved the price this year. Whether it stays there depends on the wire more than the mines, and I lean toward the wire.
