On September 8, 2025, about 800,000 tonnes of wet mud moved through the Grasberg Block Cave in Papua and killed seven workers. Freeport-McMoRan (FCX) declared force majeure on the 24th, cut 2026 guidance for the world's second-largest copper mine by roughly 35 percent, and is still not at full rates. That was the third blow of the year. In May a seismic event flooded the Kakula mine in Congo and Ivanhoe (IVN) withdrew its guidance; its 2026 target is 380,000 to 420,000 tonnes from a complex meant to do 550,000. On July 31 a collapse at El Teniente in Chile killed six workers; Codelco suspended the mine's deep expansion this August over seismic risk, and an avalanche there killed two more on October 11. Escondida, the largest copper mine on earth, shut after a fatal accident on September 23.

Benchmark put the Grasberg loss alone at 591,000 tonnes through the end of 2026, 2.6 percent of a 23-million-tonne world. Global mine output fell 1 percent in the first seven months of this year and Chilean output fell 7 percent. Smelters short of concentrate are paying miners $272 a tonne to take it; in a normal year the money flows the other way. Copper began 2026 at $12,423 a tonne, printed $14,875 on September 10, settled a record $14,822 for cash on October 12, and sits near $14,500 this week, up a third on a year ago.

People say South America controls this market, which holds until you count. Chile mined 5.3 million tonnes in 2025 and Peru 2.7 million: 35 percent of the world together, and Congo, at 3.2 million, now out-produces Peru. What Chile has is a toll booth. Codelco, state-owned, digs about a quarter of the country's copper, booked 26,875 tonnes in 2025 that an internal audit found it had not produced, and mined 11 percent less in the first half of 2026. The royalty law in force since January 2024 pushes the effective tax burden on large miners to about 45 percent. Mining is 57 percent of Chile's exports. A country that dependent on one metal does not control the price. It prays to it.

Now the question I started with: what the shortage did to renewables. Less than you would think, and arithmetic says why. An electric car carries about 53 kilograms of copper to a petrol car's 22. At $14.50 a kilogram that is $770 of copper in the EV, and the whole rally of the past year added about $190 to it. Offshore wind uses about eight tonnes per megawatt against roughly one for a coal plant, so a megawatt of turbine carries $116,000 of copper, a rounding error on hardware costing millions per megawatt installed. The transition did not get expensive because copper did. Copper got expensive because the transition is real, because a data centre needs 20 to 40 tonnes per megawatt, and because China's grid spending rose 37 percent in the first quarter.

Which is what the climate deck and the mining deck both avoid saying. Solar, wind, gas, nuclear and the server hall disagree about everything except the conductor. Whatever makes the electron, copper carries it, with a resistivity only silver beats; aluminium, the one substitute, needs about 60 percent more cross-section to do the same job. The energy transition is a copper trade wearing a climate costume, and the costume is what gets the subsidy.

One caveat on the record. More than 700,000 tonnes sit in US warehouses, pulled in ahead of a Section 232 tariff decision on refined copper that has been pending since June and that Macquarie expects to pass New Year unanswered. COMEX stocks are up roughly 700 percent since February 2025 and forecasters still pencil in a small 2026 surplus. Some of the all-time high is metal hiding in American sheds.

Three accidents moved the price. The wire is what holds it there.
