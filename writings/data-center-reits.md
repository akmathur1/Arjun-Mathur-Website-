After the close on October 29 Digital Realty (DLR) reported the best quarter a data-centre landlord has ever reported. Revenue of $1.9 billion, up 21 percent. New leases worth $855 million a year of rent, a record. A backlog of signed leases not yet paying, $2.5 billion, also a record. Guidance raised, dividend covered, a Moody's upgrade in hand. The stock rose less than two percent after hours, to about $186, which is 22 times this year's core funds from operations for a company whose tenants cannot sign fast enough.

Read the same release as a creditor and it looks different. Of the $627 million of new rent at Digital Realty's own share, $474 million, three quarters, came from hyperscale deals above a megawatt; the three largest were 60 megawatts or more each. Management offers as diversification that six different hyperscalers have been the top signer over the last seven quarters, which is another way of saying six customers drive the business. The weighted average lease it signed runs about sixteen years. It takes seventeen months from signature to the first rent cheque. The development pipeline, about 1.5 gigawatts under construction, is 63 percent pre-leased, so the other 37 percent is being built on faith, and the company will spend $4.25 to $4.75 billion on it this year, against $2.9 billion of shares sold into the market since January and about $19.5 billion of debt outstanding.

Nobody puts the mismatch on a slide. The landlord signs for sixteen years. The tenant depreciates the hardware inside the building over five or six, and the only reason to want a 60-megawatt hall in Indianapolis is that a capital budget in Seattle or Mountain View says so this year. Jefferies counted more than 80 percent of the S&P 500's gain this year, through May, as coming from AI names; without them the index was up about two percent. The tenants' appetite for halls is a function of that same enthusiasm. A REIT is being paid rent by the boom and has promised its own lenders the rent will keep arriving long after the chips in the racks are scrap.

In Rust the compiler refuses a reference that outlives the value it points at. A sixteen-year lease on a hall whose tenant's reason to occupy it is written off in five is exactly that reference. Real estate has no borrow checker; it has a credit cycle, and the check runs at the worst possible moment.

Then there is power, which the landlord does not own. Digital Realty's own call mentioned the long queues and constraints across Ohio and the nine-cent power in Indiana it moved toward instead. A data centre is a transformer with an office attached; the asset the tenant actually needs is the utility interconnection, which the REIT rents from a monopoly on terms it does not set. An analyst on the call put the build cost at roughly $15 million a megawatt. That is the price of a building whose value depends on a wire the builder does not control and a tenant whose budget it does not see.

I am not short. Shorting a landlord with a $2.5 billion backlog and investment-grade tenants is how people lose money arguing with a cycle. I own the tenants instead: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta. If the capex holds, they get the compute and I get the earnings. If it does not, they stop signing, and the stopping shows up first in the backlog of whoever built the shell. Equinix (EQIX) runs the other model, thousands of tenants renting cabinets and cross-connects rather than six renting halls, and it is the version of this business I would look at first if I looked at all. The newer single-tenant landlords are Digital Realty's trade with fewer customers.

The landlord has lent a building for sixteen years to a reason that lasts five.
