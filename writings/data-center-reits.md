After the close on October 29, Digital Realty (DLR) reported what I think is the best quarter a data-centre landlord has ever reported. Revenue of $1.9 billion, up 21 percent. New leases worth $855 million a year of rent, a record. A backlog of signed leases not yet paying of $2.5 billion, also a record. Guidance raised, dividend covered, a Moody's upgrade in hand. The stock rose less than two percent after hours, to about $186, which is 22 times this year's core funds from operations for a company whose tenants can't sign fast enough.

I read the same release a second time pretending I was a lender, and it looked different. Of the $627 million of new rent at Digital Realty's own share, $474 million, three quarters of it, came from hyperscale deals above a megawatt, and the three largest were 60 megawatts or more each. Management offers it as diversification that six different hyperscalers have been the top signer over the last seven quarters. Another way to say that is six customers drive the business. The weighted average lease it signed runs about sixteen years. It takes seventeen months from signature to the first rent cheque. The development pipeline, about 1.5 gigawatts under construction, is 63 percent pre-leased, so the other 37 percent is being built on faith, and the company will spend $4.25 to $4.75 billion on it this year, against $2.9 billion of shares sold into the market since January and about $19.5 billion of debt outstanding.

Nobody puts the mismatch on a slide, so here it is. The landlord signs for sixteen years. The tenant depreciates the hardware inside the building over five or six, and the only reason anyone wants a 60-megawatt hall in Indianapolis is that a capital budget in Seattle or Mountain View says so this year. Jefferies counted more than 80 percent of the S&P 500's gain this year, through May, as coming from AI names, and without them the index was up about two percent. The tenants' appetite for halls comes from that same enthusiasm. So you have a REIT being paid rent by the boom, which has promised its own lenders the rent will keep arriving long after the chips in the racks are scrap.

The Rust compiler refuses a reference that outlives the value it points at. A sixteen-year lease on a hall whose tenant's reason to be there gets written off in five is pretty much that reference. Real estate doesn't have a borrow checker. It has a credit cycle, and the check runs at the worst possible moment.

Then there's power, which the landlord doesn't own either. Digital Realty's own call mentioned the long queues and constraints across Ohio and the nine-cent power in Indiana it moved toward instead. A data centre is a transformer with an office attached. The asset the tenant actually needs is the utility interconnection, which the REIT rents from a monopoly on terms it doesn't set. An analyst on the call put the build cost at roughly $15 million a megawatt. That's the price of a building whose value depends on a wire the builder doesn't control and a tenant whose budget it never sees.

I'm not short, to be clear. Shorting a landlord with a $2.5 billion backlog and investment-grade tenants is how people lose money arguing with a cycle. I own the tenants instead: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta. If the capex holds, they get the compute and I get the earnings. If it doesn't, they stop signing, and the stopping shows up first in the backlog of whoever built the shell. Equinix (EQIX) runs the other model, thousands of tenants renting cabinets and cross-connects rather than six renting halls, and if I were going to look at a data-centre REIT at all that's the one I'd start with. The newer single-tenant landlords are Digital Realty's trade with even fewer customers.

Maybe the leases all get honoured and I've talked myself out of a good stock. That has happened before. But I keep coming back to the fact that the landlord has lent a building for sixteen years to a reason that lasts five, and nobody on the call seemed to think that was worth a question.
