The United States had about 16,000 golf courses at the end of 2025, roughly 2,000 fewer than at the peak twenty years ago, and the people who play on them set a record anyway. Rounds passed 500 million for the sixth year running, up a little over 1 percent on 2024. On-course golfers passed 29 million, a million more than the year before and the eighth straight year of growth, with 4 million juniors, the most since 2004, and 8 million women, an all-time high. Another 19 million people only hit balls at a range or a simulator, and 21 million more told the National Golf Foundation they didn't play in 2025 but would very much like to.

I think the equipment makers are the better way to own that, and that the bigger gain is still ahead of them. A course is a fixed asset with a fixed number of tee times, and the count is shrinking. A golfer buys balls every month, a driver every few years, shoes, gloves, and a bag, and needs no tee time for any of it. When players grow faster than the places to play, the money shows up in the pro shop and on the website. Two public companies sell most of the bag: Acushnet (GOLF), which owns Titleist and FootJoy, and Callaway Golf Company (CALY), which in January sold 60 percent of Topgolf for about half what it paid in 2020 and went back to being a club and ball company.

The ball is the best business in golf. A dozen Pro V1s costs about $55 to $58 and lasts a good player a few rounds, and Titleist had 72 percent of the ball count on the professional tours last year. In the September quarter, with everything else at Acushnet shrinking, Titleist ball revenue grew 3.3 percent to $209 million on higher prices and higher volume. Acushnet sold $831 million of balls in 2025, two and a half times Callaway, the next biggest.

The quarter both companies just reported is why the stocks are where they are. Acushnet's sales fell 1.3 percent, Callaway's fell 5.6 percent, and the shares sit at $85 against a 52-week high of $120, and $17 against $20. The sell side reads that as the pandemic cohort finally putting the clubs away. I read the same releases differently. Acushnet's nine-month sales are up 6.8 percent and its adjusted profit is up 17 percent, and the quarter fell because the new metalwoods launched in the second quarter this year instead of the third. Callaway's gross margin widened five points, it raised full-year guidance, and it holds $434 million of cash against $76 million of debt after paying down $1.4 billion in the first half. Both spent 2025 and 2026 eating tariffs, $70 million this year at Acushnet and $75 million over two years at Callaway, and both are getting part of it back now that the Supreme Court has struck down the emergency tariffs: $34 million so far at Acushnet, and $34 million in the quarter alone at Callaway. A headwind that turns into a refund is not a demand problem.

Acushnet trades at 23 times trailing earnings, pays a 1.2 percent dividend, and bought back $81 million of stock through September plus $52 million in October. Callaway is at 18 times next year's earnings and has bought back $135 million this year, with $70 million left. Neither is cheap, and the risk I'd name is that clubs are discretionary: a driver gets replaced when the owner feels rich, and a recession reaches the equipment counter before it reaches the tee sheet. My bet, over the next three to five years, is that a sport which added a million players a year for eight years while losing courses keeps buying more balls and clubs than the course count can explain, and that the two companies that sell them collect most of it.
