In 2007 a team from Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, and MIT put 26 people in an MRI scanner and let them shop. Each person got $20 in cash to spend. A product came up on the screen for four seconds, then its price for four seconds, then a yes or a no for four more, 80 products in all, with retail prices between $8 and $80 marked down to about a quarter so that half of them looked like a deal and half looked overpriced. One choice per session was drawn at random and counted for real, and the product was shipped within two weeks. People bought about 30 percent of what they saw.

The scans were the point. When a product someone wanted appeared, the nucleus accumbens lit up, which is the part of the brain that anticipates a reward. Four seconds later, if the price looked too high, a different region fired, the insula, which also activates when people expect physical pain, and at the same moment the medial prefrontal cortex, where gains get weighed against losses, went quiet. The researchers could tell from those signals whether a person was going to buy before the button got pressed, and the signals added to the prediction even after accounting for what people said they wanted when asked afterward. Paying hurts, the hurt shows up on a scan, and for most people it's the only budget they've got.

Economists had a name for it before the scanner confirmed it. Ofer Zellermayer called it the pain of paying in his 1996 dissertation at Carnegie Mellon, and Drazen Prelec and George Loewenstein built a theory around it in 1998. The pleasure of consuming and the pain of paying get booked against each other, and anything that pulls the payment away from the purchase, in time or in form, makes the payment hurt less. A credit card pulls it away in both directions, by a month and into a number on a statement.

Prelec and Duncan Simester tested that at MIT with real money. They ran a sealed-bid auction among Sloan students for tickets to a sold-out Celtics game, and told half the bidders in advance they'd settle in cash and the other half they'd settle by credit card. Nothing else about the auction differed. The card group's average bid came in at roughly double the cash group's. Prelec's line about it was that the psychological cost of spending a dollar on a credit card is only fifty cents, and they titled the paper Always Leave Home Without It, which for a marketing journal is a pretty good joke.

You can see the mechanism without a scanner. Cash gets counted out and handed over, and there's less of it in your pocket on the walk home. A card gets tapped, and the cost arrives a month later as one line among 40 on a statement, none of which feels like a decision anymore. In 2008 Priya Raghubir and Joydeep Srivastava gave people $50, as cash for some and as a gift certificate for others, and the gift certificate group spent more of it. In a second experiment, asking people to price a Thanksgiving dinner item by item, instead of as one total, closed the gap between the cash and the card answers. In 2011 Manoj Thomas at Cornell and two colleagues went through six months of grocery receipts from 1,000 households and found that the baskets paid for with a card held a larger share of the impulsive, unhealthy items than the cash baskets did. Their explanation was the same one. The cash shopper feels the cookies at the register and the card shopper doesn't.

Every payment product since has been built to take the remaining feeling out. A card used to need a signature, then a chip and a wait, then a tap. A phone needs a double-click on the side button and a glance. Amazon patented buying with one click in 1999, the patent has expired, and now everyone has it. Buy now, pay later splits a $60 purchase into four $15 pieces, three of which leave the account after the thing has arrived. The Fed's annual diary of how Americans pay, published in May, has the average person making 47 payments a month, six of them in cash. People 55 and older still make ten cash payments a month. People between 18 and 24 make two. Credit card balances were $1.26 trillion at the end of June, and the accounts that carry a balance pay an average of 22.15 percent on it.

I should be fair about the size of the effect, because it's smaller than the Celtics auction suggests. A 2021 paper ran four studies with 692 people and couldn't reproduce the credit card effect at all, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 392 results from 71 papers found it real but small, fading over the years, and strongest for things people buy to be seen with. My guess is that once the card became the default, it stopped carrying a signal. Prelec went back into the scanner in 2021 with 27 people who brought their own credit card and at least $50 of their own cash, shopping for 84 items that all cost under $20, and found something that fits. The card didn't quiet the insula, and it did light up the striatum, the reward circuit, the moment the card appeared on screen and before the price did, and the card purchases stopped tracking the price while the cash purchases kept tracking it. The paper's framing was that the card steps on the gas instead of releasing the brake. Either way the price mattered less once the card was on the screen.

So the fix is to put the feeling back at the checkout, and I've been doing that on purpose for a while. I take out a set amount of cash on Monday for the categories I overspend in, which for me is food out and the small stuff under $30, and when it's gone the category is closed until the next Monday. Groceries and gas go on debit, where the money leaves the account the same day. The credit card is for flights, hotels, and anything I might want a chargeback on, and it gets paid in full the week the statement lands. I deleted the saved cards from my browser, so buying something online means getting up to find my wallet and typing sixteen digits, and about a third of the time I don't bother. Before I tap for anything over $100 I say the price under my breath, which sounds ridiculous and is the cheapest version of Raghubir's itemizing trick I know.

There's a bonus I didn't expect. A 2016 study sold people a $6.95 coffee mug for $2, some for cash and some by card, and two hours later asked what they'd sell it back for. The cash buyers wanted nearly $3 more, and they said they felt more attached to the mug. I've noticed the same thing with the cash I spend on dinner, which I remember, and the taps on the card, which I don't. I'd rather feel $40 leave my hand on a Tuesday than find it in December on a statement with 30 other lines I don't remember agreeing to.
