Every power line on earth, strung end to end, runs to about 80 million kilometres. The IEA's count of what has to be added or replaced by 2040 to meet the targets governments have already signed is also 80 million kilometres. Not a share of it. The grid, again, in fourteen years, while the first one stays switched on.

The grid is the oldest machine most countries still run at full load. It has delivered power for over a century, and for most of this one it was treated as finished: global grid investment sat flat at roughly $300 billion a year while everyone argued about generation. The IEA's grids report said the figure had to pass $600 billion by 2030. Its 2026 investment report has network spending at about $550 billion this year, up nearly a fifth in twelve months. The money, for once, is arriving.

What is not arriving is the equipment. A large power transformer now takes up to four years to secure. A high-voltage cable takes two to three, a direct-current cable more than five, and transformer prices are up about 75 percent since 2019 while cable prices have roughly doubled. A new high-voltage line takes seven years or more; a data centre takes one to three. More than 2,500 gigawatts of generation, storage and large loads are stalled in connection queues worldwide, about 150 gigawatts of them data centres. In the whole of the last decade the world built about 1.5 million kilometres of new transmission.

Here is the physics of the mismatch, and I think it is the whole story. Any driven system has a relaxation time, and one forced faster than it can relax never settles into a new equilibrium; it lags the drive. A grid relaxes over five to fifteen years. The thing driving it, a hyperscaler's capital budget, changes by the quarter. The lag has a name on a utility's books. It is called the interconnection queue.

Now the demand the wire is supposed to carry. The world used about 28,200 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025, three percent more than the year before, and the IEA expects 33,600 by 2030: roughly 1,100 terawatt-hours a year of new demand, against 700 a year over the previous decade. The United States uses about 4,200. So the world is adding a United States of demand every four years, and in the IEA's central scenario it adds more than two of them by 2035. Most people assume that is the AI. Globally it is not; data centres are less than a tenth of the growth, and the peak is set by air conditioning. America is different: data centres are about half of the 420-plus terawatt-hours it is set to add by 2030.

Which is where American Electric Power (AEP) comes in. Its summer peak was about 37 gigawatts last year. By May it had signed agreements for 63 gigawatts of new load by 2030, 41 of them in Texas, most of it data centres. They came to AEP because it owns about 2,100 miles of 765-kilovolt line, nine tenths of what exists in the country. Its five-year capital plan rose a third to $72 billion last October, to $78 billion in May and to $100 billion this week, with the rate base compounding at 13 percent or better through 2031. What the release calls "up to $21 billion of fixed cost offsets for residential customers" translates: under the old tariffs you paid for the hyperscaler's substation; under the new ones in Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky and West Virginia, the hyperscaler does. Last October AEP guided residential rates up 3.5 percent a year, and it earns a regulated return on every dollar of either. If I owned a utility, it would be the one that owns the wire everyone else is waiting for.

The power crosses the state in milliseconds. The wire it needs arrives in 2033.
