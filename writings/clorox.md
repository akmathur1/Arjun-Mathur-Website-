Clorox (CLX) closed at $239.87 on August 5, 2020. That was the week it reported the fastest sales growth in its history, with every canister of disinfecting wipes in America sold before it reached a shelf. On October 28 this year it closed at $87.36, after touching $79.12 on October 1. It's 64 percent below a record that is six years old, and the whole company is worth about $10.5 billion. Nothing about the product changed. Bleach is sodium hypochlorite in water and it does the same thing to a kitchen counter now that it did in 2019. What changed was how scared people were, and in 2020 fear was the only thing the price was measuring.

The pitch I still hear for this stock is that household products are where you hide. Usually someone cites a bank study showing home-goods companies beat the S&P 500 in volatile periods. I went looking for that study and couldn't find it, but I don't think it's needed, because the public record says the same thing. In 2022 the S&P 500 returned minus 18.5 percent and consumer staples lost less than one percent. In February this year, with tech selling off, staples led the index by 13 points. Clorox has a beta of 0.57. When the market falls, this stock falls less. I'm not disputing any of that.

What the pitch leaves out is what the hiding place costs in the years without a crash, which is most of them. The staples trade is basically a put option with an expensive premium. Over the twelve months to October 2025 the sector returned minus 0.7 percent against 15.5 for the index. Clorox is down 27 percent in a year when the market went up. Its 52-week high of $128.90 came in February, during the volatility, and by October the volatility had passed and so had the bid.

The company has also had a rough few years at the one thing it's supposed to be good at, which is being boring. In August 2023 a cyberattack took down its order systems, slowed its factories and knocked about $356 million off a quarter's sales, roughly a fifth. In 2025 it moved its US business onto a new ERP system, the kind of migration that's supposed to be invisible. Retailers stocked up ahead of the switch and then ran inventories down, and Clorox says that cost fiscal 2026 7.5 points of sales and 90 cents of earnings. Net sales fell 5 percent to $6.72 billion and earnings fell 26 percent to $4.81 a share. Then in April it closed the $2.25 billion purchase of GOJO, the maker of Purell, mostly with debt, which takes borrowings to about $5 billion. A company that peaked on pandemic hygiene has just paid two billion dollars for more pandemic hygiene.

The dividend is the one thing that has behaved. Clorox has raised it for 48 consecutive years by the count I found (I've only seen that figure in one place, so take it as roughly right), most recently by a cent to $1.25 a quarter. At $87 the yield is 5.7 percent and the payout is 103 percent of trailing earnings. That ratio comes down if the ERP comparison washes out and GOJO adds the 9.5 points of sales promised for fiscal 2027. It doesn't come down if the CEO's own word for the operating environment, "challenging," turns out to be the accurate one.

So what is this stock? My best description is a phase-change artifact. In 2020 fear pushed the market across a boundary where the only thing anyone wanted to hold was the thing that kills germs, and Clorox got priced as if that state were permanent. It wasn't, as it turned out. The system relaxed, the premium evaporated, and what's left is a slow-growing bleach company at 18 times trailing earnings with debt it didn't have before.

I don't own it. I wouldn't short it either, because a 5.7 percent yield and a 0.57 beta will beat the index in the next crash, and there will be one. But the safe trade is only safe for the quarter when nobody wants anything else. The other nineteen quarters it's a stock like any other, with worse growth, and you pay for the insurance in every one of them.
