Clorox (CLX) closed at $239.87 on August 5, 2020, the week it reported the fastest sales growth in its history, with every canister of disinfecting wipes in America sold before it reached the shelf. It closed at $87.36 on October 28, touched $79.12 on October 1, and sits 64 percent below a record six years old, worth about $10.5 billion. The product did not change. Bleach is sodium hypochlorite in water, and it did the same thing to a kitchen counter in 2019 as it does today. What changed was the fear, and fear was the only thing the 2020 price measured.

The thesis people still carry about this stock is that household products are the place to hide. The version I keep hearing cites a bank study showing home-goods companies beat the S&P 500 in volatile periods. I could not find the study and do not need it; the public record says the same thing more precisely. In 2022 the S&P 500 returned minus 18.5 percent and consumer staples lost less than one percent. In February this year, with technology selling off, staples led the index by 13 points. Clorox carries a beta of 0.57. When the market falls, this stock falls less. That part is true.

Here is the part the pitch omits. The staples trade is a put option with an expensive premium, paid in every year without a crash. Over the twelve months to October 2025 the sector returned minus 0.7 percent against 15.5 for the index. Clorox is down 27 percent in a year when the market rose. Its 52-week high, $128.90, came in February, during the volatility; by October the volatility had passed and so had the bid.

And the company has had a bad run at being boring. In August 2023 a cyberattack took down its order systems, slowed its factories and cut a quarter's sales by about $356 million, roughly a fifth. In 2025 it moved its American business onto a new ERP system, the kind of migration that is supposed to be invisible; retailers stocked up ahead of the switch and then ran inventories down, which Clorox says cost fiscal 2026 7.5 points of sales and 90 cents of earnings. Net sales fell 5 percent to $6.72 billion, earnings fell 26 percent to $4.81 a share. In April it closed the $2.25 billion purchase of GOJO, the maker of Purell, mostly with debt, taking borrowings to about $5 billion. A company that peaked on pandemic hygiene has just paid two billion dollars for more of it.

The dividend is the one thing that has behaved. Clorox has raised it for 48 consecutive years, most recently by a cent to $1.25 a quarter; at $87 the yield is 5.7 percent and the payout is 103 percent of trailing earnings. That ratio comes down if the ERP comparison washes out and GOJO adds the 9.5 points of sales promised for fiscal 2027; it does not if the chief executive's own word for the operating environment, "challenging," is the honest one.

So what is this stock? A phase-change artifact. In 2020 fear pushed the market across a boundary where the only thing anyone wanted to hold was the thing that kills germs, and Clorox was priced as if that state were permanent. Such states are not. The system relaxed, the premium evaporated, and what remained was a slow-growing bleach company at 18 times trailing earnings with debt it did not have before.

I do not own it. I would not short it; a 5.7 percent yield and a 0.57 beta will beat the index in the next crash, and there will be one. But the safe trade is safe for the quarter when nobody wants anything else. For the other nineteen quarters it is a stock like any other, with worse growth.

Safety is a price you pay every day for a day you cannot schedule.
