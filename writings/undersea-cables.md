On Saturday, September 6, 2025, two cables on the floor of the Red Sea off Jeddah stopped carrying light. One was SEA-ME-WE-4, the other IMEWE, both trunk lines between the Indian subcontinent and Europe, and a third, FALCON, went down with them. Within hours Microsoft was warning Azure customers about traffic routed through the Middle East, Cloudflare was measuring delays of up to 30 percent between India and Europe, and Pakistan and the Gulf were running slow. The International Cable Protection Committee's early read was a merchant ship dragging its anchor, and nobody has shown otherwise. Repairs were put at two to six weeks, because the ships need permits from several governments and have to cross a war zone to get there.

The physical object is smaller than most people picture. Across most of an ocean a submarine cable is about the width of a garden hose, and the fibres inside it are the width of a hair. There are 607 of them in service by TeleGeography's count this September, with another 102 planned, something over 1.5 million kilometres in all. The ICPC logs 150 to 200 faults a year, and 70 to 80 percent of them are fishing gear and anchors in shallow water. Sabotage is rare. It doesn't need to be common, though. The Red Sea carries about a quarter of the data between Asia and Europe through roughly fifteen cables threaded past one strait, and one anchor, if that's what it was, took out three of them on a Saturday.

The arithmetic from there gets uncomfortable. Satellites carry well under one percent of international traffic; the FCC's figure for the United States is 0.37 percent. Everything else, every cloud region and every set of model weights, is a wire on the seabed, and the wires increasingly belong to four companies. Google has stakes in more than 30 systems, Meta in about 20, and TeleGeography counts Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon as stakeholders in over two-thirds of the systems now planned. Before the 1990s cables were built by consortia of national carriers, AT&T, British Telecom, Telstra. Now the cloud lays its own. A National Bureau of Economic Research paper this year puts about 70 percent of global data across at least one American-owned cable, up from under 20 percent in the early nineties. People argue about whether the internet is centralised. On the seabed it always has been.

The laying is more concentrated still. Three firms build nearly everything: Alcatel Submarine Networks, 80 percent of which the French state bought from Nokia in 2024 for about €100 million; SubCom, the only American turnkey supplier, which Cerberus bought for $325 million in 2018 and which reached a record $4.7 billion backlog by 2024; and NEC. China's HMN Tech has most of the rest. Then there's the part nobody budgets for. Roughly 60 ships on earth can lay or mend a cable, and fewer than 20 of them do only repair. Eight of the sixty are under eighteen years old, nineteen are over thirty, the oldest dates from 1978, and the study TeleGeography wrote for the SubOptic Association last year has two-thirds of the maintenance fleet at the end of its working life by 2040. Replacing it would cost about $3 billion over fifteen years, which is what four cloud companies spend on data centres in a day and a half.

The owners of the cables are the people who least want you to think about them, because a cloud that's a wire is a cloud with a location. In May Iranian officials floated charging a fee on the cables under the Strait of Hormuz and putting their repair in Iranian hands, and an IRGC official has reportedly put the cables on the target list. Those lines carry less than one percent of global bandwidth. One repair ship sits inside the Gulf.

So nearly everything that crosses an ocean does it inside a garden hose, guarded by sixty ships and an assumption. I'd never thought about the ships before September, and now I can't stop.
