On October 15, Korean brokerages sold 80.5 billion won of their customers' shares without asking them. A week earlier the daily figure had been 6.4 billion. In between, the KOSPI fell seven sessions in a row, from about 6,900 to 5,500, and the margin loans underneath it, which had gone back above 34 trillion won on October 13 for the first time since July, started shrinking the only way they shrink quickly, which is by being called.

The run-up needs dates to make sense. The KOSPI first closed above 2,000 in July 2007. It ended 2024 at 2,399, with one year above 3,000 in 2021. So seventeen years, four hundred points, and a whole literature on the Korea discount.

Then it closed above 4,000 last October, 5,000 in January, 6,000 in February, 7,000 in May, 8,000 three weeks after that, 9,000 in June, and touched 9,385 on June 19. Seven thousand points in eighteen months. It then fell 44 percent in six weeks, to 5,263 on July 29, recovered to 7,100 by September, and gave up a fifth again in the first half of October. It closed at 6,012 on October 28 and 5,938 on October 29. I don't think that's the chart of a stock market. It's the chart of a margin account.

The awkward thing is that the earnings are real. SK Hynix reported at the end of October: revenue of 104.6 trillion won, up 328 percent, an operating margin of 79.7 percent, memory sold out through 2029. Samsung made 107 trillion won of operating profit in the same quarter and promised 90 to 110 trillion won of dividends and buybacks. So you've got two companies sitting on top of an index in a country where more than half of last quarter's growth surprise came from chips, private consumption fell at its fastest rate in four and a half years, and bank delinquencies are at a ten-year high. The index is basically the memory cycle, and the country is a lot more than that.

The retail response to all this was to borrow. Margin loans went from 28.9 trillion won at the end of July to 34.3 trillion by October 12, up nearly a fifth, while cash sitting in brokerage accounts fell 7 percent to 96.9 trillion. A market without leverage damps its own swings, because a lower price finds a buyer. A market carrying 34 trillion won of margin amplifies them, because a lower price finds a margin clerk.

Forced sales rose 12.7-fold in a week, unsettled receivables reached 1.24 trillion won, and the margin balance dropped almost a trillion in a single day. It has since settled below 30 trillion, and on October 27 retail investors net sold 949 billion won while foreigners bought. The forced selling did what the dip couldn't and broke the loop, at least for now.

The government hasn't exactly been a bystander either. On October 29 the president's senior secretary for policy apologised in the National Assembly for single-stock leveraged ETFs, products that pay twice a stock's daily move, whose launch, as he put it, coincided with a period of repricing. Daily double leverage in a market that moves 6 percent on a Wednesday turns volatility into losses even when the stock ends up where it began, and the state waved those products through.

So I think this is a reset, and I won't pretend to know the date. What I can say is what has already happened: the index has been cut 44 percent once and 20 percent once inside four months, and the forced selling that did the second cut started with margin debt at 34 trillion won. The next leg begins wherever the margin balance sits when memory prices stop rising, and memory prices have never not stopped rising. Hynix says the shortage runs through 2030. The whole trade is a bet on that word, through.

The photograph above these stories is usually the same one, a wall of screens in the dealing room at Hana Bank's head office in Jung-gu, showing the KOSPI and the KOSDAQ. The screens show the index. I've never seen one that showed the loans.
