On a stretch of coast in Central Sulawesi that was grassland in 2013 there is now a 2,000-hectare industrial park with about 85,000 workers, fifty-odd tenants, a two-gigawatt coal plant and $34.3 billion of mostly Chinese capital in the ground. The Morowali park is what a policy looks like when it works. In 2020 Indonesia banned the export of raw nickel ore: if you wanted the rock you had to build the furnace next to it, and the Chinese did. Indonesia mined just over 30 percent of the world's nickel that year. In 2025 it mined 2.6 million of the world's 3.9 million tonnes, two of every three.

Everyone else shrank to fit. Australian output fell 54 percent last year; BHP (BHP) mothballed Nickel West in October 2024 and has promised only a review by February 2027. Philippine output fell 24 percent. Glencore (GLEN) guides to 70,000 to 80,000 tonnes this year, ten days of Indonesia. The Indonesians did it with high-pressure acid leach plants built for under $1.5 billion apiece and ramped inside a year; the Western plants of the last cycle, Goro and Ambatovy, cost nearly $6 billion each and took five years or more.

Which brings me to the price, which everyone assumes is high. It is not. Nickel averaged $15,000 a tonne on the LME in 2025, down 11 percent on 2024. The monopolist flooded its own market. So in February Jakarta did what a cartel does and cut the 2026 ore quota to 260 to 270 million wet tonnes, about a third below what it had approved for 2025. The metal ran from $14,000 in December to $20,000 on May 6, the highest since 2024. Then Indonesia's imports of Philippine ore rose 66 percent, smelter utilisation fell from 84 to 76 percent, lines in Sulawesi dropped below half capacity, and by October the ministry was cutting its ore benchmark and readying supplementary quotas. The International Nickel Study Group flipped its 2026 call from a 32,000-tonne deficit to a 19,000-tonne surplus. Nickel closed at $15,605 on October 28, roughly its level a year ago, with 279,000 tonnes in LME sheds. The rally round-tripped in ten months.

Indonesia owns the ore and China owns the furnaces, and when they disagree the furnace wins, because ore left in the ground costs nothing and a smelter left cold still owes the bank. The quota was set at compile time and renegotiated at runtime. Jakarta cannot run OPEC on an industry whose members are its own largest investors, and the investors have noticed: Tsingshan is pitching a park in Madagascar, Lygend wants a stake in Tanzania's Kabanga, and foreign direct investment into Indonesia fell 6 percent in 2025. Last week the Chinese-owned plants were reportedly discussing halving their own intermediate output. The producers are doing the rationing the state could not.

The cost of the boom is paid where the quota does not reach. In June 2025 a Greenpeace video of stripped islands in Raja Ampat, an archipelago holding about three quarters of the world's known coral species at the heart of the Coral Triangle, drew fifteen million views, and the president revoked four of the five permits there within days. The fifth belonged to Gag Nikel, a subsidiary of the state miner. Its suspension was lifted in September.

The Western answer is Talon Metals' (TLO) Tamarack deposit in Minnesota: 8.6 million indicated tonnes at 1.73 percent, Rio Tinto as partner, a Tesla offtake, $20.6 million from the Pentagon and a state scoping decision still pending. The company's own plan is production this decade, if the permits come. America imports 41 percent of the nickel it uses and nearly all of the primary metal, led by Canada and Norway, and that will not change because a Jakarta ministry changed a number.

A cartel of one is a country arguing with itself, and the furnaces are winning.
