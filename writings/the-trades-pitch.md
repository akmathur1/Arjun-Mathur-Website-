In September 2025 the chief executive of Nvidia sat in a British television studio and told the country's teenagers what to do with their lives. "If you're an electrician, you're a plumber, a carpenter, we're going to need hundreds of thousands of them to build all of these factories." The skilled craft segment of every economy, he said, "is going to see a boom." He said it again on Fox Business this August: hundreds of thousands of jobs for "people who build things, that make things with their hands." BlackRock's chief executive told an energy conference in March 2025 that he had warned the White House "we're going to run out of electricians." Blackstone's president promised a "huge boom in blue-collar employment." In July, leaders from BlackRock, Carhartt, Ford and Google formed an Alliance for America's Skilled Trades.

I want to take the pitch seriously first, because the shortage is real. The Census Bureau put data-center construction at an $85 billion annual rate in August, up 73 percent on the year, while factory construction has fallen 32 percent from its 2024 peak. Electrical work is 45 to 70 percent of a data center's construction budget, and the electricians' union near Washington has doubled its membership since 2018 to 14,700 and still can't fill its calls. A qualified electrician there earns about $59.50 an hour, better than $120,000 a year with benefits. About 20,000 electricians retire each year. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects electricians to grow 9 percent in the decade to 2035 and plumbers 7 percent, against 3.5 percent for all jobs. None of that is what bothers me.

What bothers me is who's talking and what they're buying. Every voice in that chorus is a buyer of electrical labor or a financier of the buildings that need it. When a customer tells you to increase production, that isn't career guidance. It's a purchase order, written in the language of virtue, real work and hands and things, because a purchase order written in the language of wages would have to name a price and a term. The term is the capex cycle. Four companies will spend about $725 billion on data centers this year. That cycle will end the way capital cycles end, and the message to the eighteen-year-old doesn't come with a date on it.

Then there are the numbers the pitch leaves out. Nine percent growth for electricians is 75,900 jobs over ten years, about 7,600 a year across the whole country. In 2024 the electrical contractors' association counted 120,000 apprenticeship applications, up from 70,000 two years earlier, which is already more than the positions on offer. The pitch has worked. A large data center employs up to 1,500 people at peak construction and about 50 once it's finished, and one analysis of federal payroll data found as few as 23,000 permanent data-center jobs nationwide in 2024. The farmers who turn up in the same speeches are the one group projected to shrink, down 3 percent, and ironworkers grow 1 percent. An electrical apprenticeship takes four to five years. In a driven system, a response that takes longer to arrive than the force takes to change is always answering the previous push. In 2008 nearly one in four union construction electricians was out of work. Nobody who gave a speech in 2005 was laid off in 2009.

The equipment side has no such exposure. Caterpillar (CAT) sells the generators behind every server hall, Deere (DE) sells the tractor to a farmer the projections say won't be replaced, and Otis (OTIS) will service the elevators whichever tenant is in the building. Machines get bought once, at the top of the cycle. Labor gets rented while the cycle lasts and handed back when it ends.

So, become an electrician if the work suits you. It's a good trade and I mean that. Just notice that the man on television isn't your guidance counsellor. He's your customer, and he'd like the price to come down.
