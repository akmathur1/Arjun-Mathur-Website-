In February Paramount wired Netflix (NFLX) $2.8 billion for the privilege of buying Warner Bros. Discovery instead of it. Netflix had agreed in December to take the studio and HBO for $82.7 billion, then went all-cash at $27.75 a share in January; Paramount came over the top at $31, about $111 billion for all of Warner rather than just the studios, and Warner's board took it. Netflix declined to raise. The deal closed on October 6 and the combined company is called Skydance. Netflix walked away with the break fee, no $83 billion acquisition to digest, and a share price that has since fallen through the floor.

I own a large position. The stock closed at $62.05 on October 29, a 52-week low, down about 44 percent in a year from a high of $116.73. The quarter that did the damage, reported October 20, was a beat: revenue of $12.9 billion up 12 percent, operating margin 34.6 percent against 28.2 a year ago, earnings up 47 percent, $4.8 billion of buybacks, the most ever. It was punished for cutting its 2026 advertising forecast from about $3 billion to about $2.7 billion, and for a line in the letter admitting "some investor concern about our view hours growth."

Hold those two numbers next to each other. Three hundred million dollars of ads arriving a few quarters late, against a company that will do $51 billion of revenue this year at a 31.5 percent margin and generate $12 billion of free cash. The market took about $200 billion off the valuation for that.

The war is over and Netflix won it. The world holds 2.24 billion online video subscriptions and spent $176 billion on them last year, passing pay television for the first time. Netflix had more than 325 million of those paying households at the end of last year, by far the largest, and it is the only pure streamer earning a margin a packaged-goods company would envy. The rivals that remain are divisions of a phone maker, a retailer and a theme-park operator, plus Skydance, which lined up $54 billion of debt commitments to assemble a library. The American household holds about four paid video services and pays about $69 a month for them, and 73 percent say they are tired of the price rises. Churn across the industry runs near 40 percent a year. Netflix's churn after this year's increases was better than it forecast.

Which brings me to the thing I actually want, and the irony in wanting it. Pershing Square bought about 3.1 million Netflix shares in January 2022 and sold them in April after a subscriber miss, booking a loss above $400 million; its founder wrote that he had lost confidence in his ability to predict the company. The shares then rose roughly fivefold before the split. I would like Pershing Square, or anyone with its temperament and more patience, back on the register. The argument is not complicated. Netflix spends about 1.1 times its amortisation on content and does not need to spend more; it needs to say so, cap the budget, and return the rest. It raised prices in the United States, Mexico and Spain this year and the members stayed. It has $22.3 billion of buyback authority and a share price it can retire at about 20 times this year's earnings. The content arms race ended on October 6. Somebody should tell the capital budget.

Uber (UBER), which I also own, is the same trade in a different costume. Both went from growth at any price to disciplined cash machines with an advertising layer bolted on. Both generate $10 to $12 billion of free cash a year. Both sit at 52-week lows because the market, having spent a decade demanding they grow up, has decided grown-up companies are boring.

Netflix was paid $2.8 billion to lose. The market has charged it $200 billion for winning.
