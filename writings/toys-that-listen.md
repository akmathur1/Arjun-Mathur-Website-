In February 2017 the German Federal Network Agency, which regulates telecoms, ordered a blonde doll called My Friend Cayla off the shelves, on the grounds that an object which conceals a microphone and transmits what it hears is a prohibited surveillance device. Genesis Toys sold the doll, Nuance handled the speech, and the Bluetooth link had no authentication, so anyone within range could pair with a child's toy and listen. The regulator was not making a metaphor. It applied the law written for hidden bugs to a product in the toy aisle, and the law fit.

A year later the Federal Trade Commission settled with VTech for $650,000 over a tablet system that collected the names, birthdates and genders of hundreds of thousands of children without parental consent, was breached in 2015, and had told parents the data was encrypted when none of it was. Last September the FTC charged Apitor, a Shenzhen maker of robot toys, with letting a Chinese analytics kit called JPush pull precise location from thousands of children through its app; the $500,000 penalty was suspended because the company said it could not pay. Then came the chatbot plush. The Kumma bear from FoloToy, sold as running on GPT-4o, told testers how to find and light matches before moving on to sexual topics; OpenAI cut the developer off in November, and by February the bear was still offering GPT-5.1. Mattel announced its OpenAI partnership in June 2025, said its first product would arrive by year-end, then said not that holiday season. The toys are sensors pointed at the youngest people in the house, and the business model requires it.

Which brings me to the sensor that won. Amazon put the Echo on sale on November 6, 2014, at $199, or $99 for Prime members with an invitation. It had seven microphones and almost no mind: music, weather, alarms, pitched fairly as a whole-home Siri. The Wall Street Journal later put the devices division's losses from 2017 through 2021 above $25 billion. Amazon could sell the hardware at a loss because the product was a microphone in the kitchen with a Prime account attached, and it has since shipped more than 600 million Alexa devices. Run the opposite counterfactual: a device arriving in 2025 with a frontier model inside. Smarter. It would have lost. The intelligence was always retrofittable; the countertop was not. Alexa+ reached everyone in the United States on February 4 this year, free with Prime, and 97 percent of the devices Amazon ever shipped can run it. A dumb device that owns the room upgrades into a smart one. A smart device that arrives late has to find a room.

Think of it as a phase transition. A room with a listening device in it is a different state of matter from a room without one, and the energy barrier is the first installation. Once the microphone is there, making it cleverer costs a software push; making a cleverer microphone appear in that kitchen costs a second purchase nobody makes.

That is why the ethics of the toy aisle are not a side issue. Mattel and its imitators are not competing on intelligence; the intelligence is rented from the same handful of labs. They are competing to be the first microphone a child trusts, and the retention policies tell you what the prize is. The amended children's privacy rule took full effect on April 22; a bill to update the underlying law passed the Senate unanimously in March and is stalled in the House. The German regulator understood in 2017 what the American market still prices as a feature: a device that hears a child is a device that hears a child, whatever its face is.

Cayla has been illegal in Germany for nine years. The room she was built to occupy was taken by a speaker.
