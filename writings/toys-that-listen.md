In February 2017 the German Federal Network Agency, which regulates telecoms, ordered a blonde doll called My Friend Cayla off the shelves, on the grounds that an object which conceals a microphone and transmits what it hears is a prohibited surveillance device. Genesis Toys sold the doll, Nuance handled the speech, and the Bluetooth link had no authentication, so anyone within range could pair with a child's toy and listen. The regulator wasn't making a metaphor. It applied the law written for hidden bugs to a product in the toy aisle, and the law fit.

A year later the Federal Trade Commission settled with VTech for $650,000 over a tablet system that collected the names, birthdates and genders of hundreds of thousands of children without parental consent, had been breached in 2015, and had told parents the data was encrypted when none of it was. Last September the FTC charged Apitor, a Shenzhen maker of robot toys, with letting a Chinese analytics kit called JPush pull precise location from thousands of children through its app; the $500,000 penalty was suspended because the company said it couldn't pay. Then came the chatbot plush. The Kumma bear from FoloToy, sold as running on GPT-4o, told testers how to find and light matches before moving on to sexual topics. OpenAI cut the developer off in November, and by February the bear was still on sale offering GPT-5.1. Mattel announced its OpenAI partnership in June 2025, said its first product would arrive by year-end, then said not that holiday season after all. These toys are sensors pointed at the youngest people in the house, and the business model requires them to be.

Which brings me to the sensor that actually won. Amazon put the Echo on sale on November 6, 2014, at $199, or $99 for Prime members with an invitation. It had seven microphones and almost no mind: music, weather, alarms, pitched fairly honestly as a whole-home Siri. The Wall Street Journal later put the devices division's losses from 2017 through 2021 above $25 billion. Amazon could sell the hardware at a loss because the product was really a microphone in the kitchen with a Prime account attached, and it has since shipped more than 600 million Alexa devices. Now imagine the opposite: a device arriving in 2025 with a frontier model inside, much smarter. I think it would have lost. The intelligence was always something you could add later, and the countertop wasn't. Alexa+ reached everyone in the United States on February 4 this year, free with Prime, and 97 percent of the devices Amazon ever shipped can run it. A dumb device that owns the room gets upgraded into a smart one. A smart device that shows up late has to go and find a room.

The way I'd describe it is as a phase transition. A room with a listening device in it is a different state of matter from a room without one, and the energy barrier is the first installation. Once the microphone is there, making it cleverer costs a software push. Making a cleverer microphone appear in that same kitchen costs a second purchase that nobody makes.

That's why I don't think the ethics of the toy aisle are a side issue. Mattel and its imitators aren't competing on intelligence, since the intelligence is rented from the same handful of labs. They're competing to be the first microphone a child trusts, and the retention policies tell you what the prize is. The amended children's privacy rule took full effect on April 22. A bill to update the underlying law passed the Senate unanimously in March and is stalled in the House. The German regulator understood in 2017 what the American market still prices as a feature: a device that hears a child is a device that hears a child, whatever face it's wearing.

Cayla has been illegal in Germany for nine years. The room she was built to occupy got taken by a speaker, and I'm not sure anyone noticed.
