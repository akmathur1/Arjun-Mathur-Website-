Lead is soft enough to bend around a corner by hand, which is why the Romans ran it under their cities and why the people who did the running were called plumbers: plumbum gave the trade its name. The United States banned new lead water pipe in 1986. By the Environmental Protection Agency's count, up to nine million homes are still connected to the main through the old stuff, and in October 2024 the agency finally wrote the rule that makes water systems dig it out: all of it, within ten years of a compliance date that lands in late 2027. The last lead service line is scheduled to leave the ground around 2037. A line laid the year before the ban will be fifty-one years old by then.

The chemistry is why this took a century. Lead does not leak into water on its own schedule. A pipe carrying properly treated water grows a passivation film on its inner wall, a thin scale of insoluble lead carbonate and phosphate between the metal and your glass, and as long as nobody touches the water chemistry the film holds. Change the source, drop the orthophosphate, let the pH drift, and the film dissolves and the metal comes with it. That is what Flint was: a passivation layer, removed. The new rule lowers the action level from 15 to 10 micrograms per litre, but a limit is a measurement, not a fix. The fix is the trench.

The economics are not close. The agency puts the cost of the rule at $1.5 to $2 billion a year and the benefits at $13 to $25 billion: up to 900,000 infants a year spared low birth weight, 1,500 fewer premature heart deaths, 200,000 IQ points not lost. Thirteen dollars back for one in. No private balance sheet is allowed to make that trade, because the dollar comes out of a water bill in 2029 and the thirteen come back as a child who reads a little better in 2045. So the pipe sat there through the 1991 rule and the 2021 rule and is only now being counted.

Here is what I find strange about water, more than roads or wires. It is the one utility where the asset is older than the institution. Roughly 67,000 water systems fall under the rule, most of them small, and the lead under their streets predates the system's charter, its ratepayers, its engineers and often the municipality itself. A power line is replaced on a depreciation schedule. A lead service line was never on one, because nobody expected to replace it; the plan was to coat it and hope.

Who pays for the trench. Washington put up $15 billion from the 2021 infrastructure law for lead lines specifically and $26 billion usable for lead at all, against an annual bill the agency itself puts near $2 billion for a decade, so the balance lands on the water bill in the towns least able to pay it. The water utilities' own trade association sued over the rule the month it was published, on the grounds that ten years is not feasible; the government has defended it through two administrations, briefing closed in April, and the court hears argument this autumn. Meanwhile the rule stands: inventories and first replacements are due November 1, 2027, and the agency issued fresh guidance in August on how to reach the pipes. It sells the programme as 15,500 jobs per billion dollars, which is true and the wrong reason. The country has about 510,000 plumbers and pipefitters for nine million trenches.

If I wanted to own the shovel rather than the hole, I would look at Pentair (PNR), which sells the pumps and filters that sit between a dubious main and a kitchen tap, and Advanced Drainage Systems (WMS), which extrudes pipe from recycled plastic for the water that falls on the street rather than the water that comes out of it. Neither has a lead problem. Both get paid when somebody decides to fix one.

The metal named the trade. So far it has outlasted every rule written about it.
