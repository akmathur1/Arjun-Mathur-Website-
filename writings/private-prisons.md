On July 2 the Department of Homeland Security paid CoreCivic (CXW) $1.5 billion for two detention centers in California, and on August 4 another $734 million for two more in Kansas and Minnesota: 7,187 beds for $2.2 billion, which the company's own release works out to $307,000 a bed. On October 5 GEO Group (GEO) sold its Adelanto complex in the Mojave, 2,644 beds, for $950 million, or $359,000 a bed. ICE's median daily rate for a bed is $92. In every case the seller stays on to run the building; GEO's contract runs to 2029 with an option to 2034. The government bought the asset and kept the operator.

The line that two companies own half of America's private prisons is roughly true and misses the point. Private prisons hold about 8 percent of state and federal prisoners, and GEO and CoreCivic are most of that trade, but prisons are not where the money is. Immigration is. About a third of ICE detainees sit in GEO facilities and roughly a quarter of ICE's beds are CoreCivic's; 86 percent of detainees were in for-profit hands when this administration took office. ICE was 48 percent of GEO's $2.63 billion of revenue last year and about 42 percent of CoreCivic's in the first quarter of this one. Between them the two booked about $4.8 billion in 2025.

What changed is one number. On January 20, 2025 ICE held 39,314 people. By July 30 this year the average daily population was 67,180, after a January peak above 73,000, and the count of authorized facilities had doubled from 134 to 272. The law signed on July 4, 2025 put $45 billion into detention through September 2029, against an annual detention appropriation that had been about $3.8 billion. GEO took its ICE capacity from about 20,000 beds to 26,000 in a year, the most new business in its history. CoreCivic now guides to $1.5 billion of net income in 2026, because selling four buildings to your largest customer books as a $1.8 billion gain. Both raised their buybacks within days of closing, GEO to $1.25 billion, CoreCivic to $1.2 billion with $500 million of it executed at once.

The other complaint is that these companies owe the government nothing. It is backwards. They are contractors, and in this contract the obligations run toward them. ICE agreements carry guaranteed minimums, beds paid for nightly whether anyone sleeps in them, and an analysis of ICE's own July data found about 8,200 such beds standing empty at more than $1.2 million a day. The GAO's September report is titled, in full, Immigration Detention: Urgent Planning Needed to Avoid Further Waste of Taxpayer Dollars. What flows the other way is harder to see. The Freedom of Information Act does not reach a private operator, and a bill to change that has been introduced since 2005 and never passed. ICE's detention standards are not written into law. The acting director of ICE since June spent about a decade as a GEO executive. Thirty-nine people died in ICE custody in the twelve months to January, the most in any year since the agency was created in 2003, twenty-seven of them in privately run facilities.

I would not own either stock, and the reason is structural before it is moral. Every business I own has a growth metric I can reason about: subscribers, payment volume, syrup. The growth metric here is the number of human beings the executive branch decides to confine, which went from 39,000 to 73,000 in a year and which the next administration, appropriations fight or court order can move the other way with a signature. GEO has traded between $12.51 and $33.17 over the past twelve months and closed at $27.57 on October 28, at thirteen times earnings. That is the chart of a referendum.

The government owns the buildings now. The companies are still paid by the bed.
