For every rupee of stock that changed hands on India's exchanges last fiscal year, about 394 rupees of derivatives changed hands on top of it. In 2016 the ratio was 14. The regulator, SEBI, publishes this number itself, in a study it put out in August with the flat title "Profitability of Individual Traders in the Equity Derivatives Segment," which is the bureaucratic way of saying they counted, and the traders lose.

And they do lose. Of the 8.75 million individuals who traded futures and options in the year to March 2026, 87.7 percent finished in the red. Together they lost 91,685 crore rupees, roughly $10 billion, and that was an improvement, because the year before 10.6 million people lost about $13 billion. Over five years the cumulative figure is 3.85 lakh crore, call it $45 billion, transferred from salaried Indians to whoever sat on the other side of the trade. About a third of these traders hold no stock at all. They aren't hedging anything. They're buying weekly Nifty options on their phones more or less the way their fathers bought lottery tickets, except that a lottery pays out in the aggregate and this doesn't.

So who was on the other side? For two years the single most profitable answer was a firm from New York that most of them had never heard of. SEBI's July 2025 order alleged that Jane Street made about 36,500 crore rupees, around $4.3 billion, from Indian index derivatives between January 2023 and March 2025, and that 4,843 crore of that came from manipulating the Bank Nifty index: buy the constituent banks hard in the morning, let the index drag the options with it, hold a much larger short options position, then sell the banks into the close. SEBI barred the firm from the market. Jane Street deposited the $567 million in escrow within two weeks, had the bar lifted within three, and in October was still arguing the merits before the Securities Appellate Tribunal, where it has accused the regulator of moving the goalposts after the fact.

I keep holding those two facts next to each other. The retail base loses on the order of $10 billion a year, and the best trading firm in the world found that market so rich that its 2024 India take, $2.3 billion, was more than a tenth of its global revenue. In 2025 Jane Street booked $39.6 billion in net trading revenue, more than JPMorgan's entire trading operation. India was part of the reason it could.

This is where I part ways with the moralists. SEBI's complaint was never that Jane Street was good at this. It was that one pattern, in one index, crossed the line from aggressive into manipulative, and that's a question for the tribunal. The structural fact is older and stranger. On top of a stock market worth roughly $5 trillion, India has built a derivatives market whose notional turnover runs at four hundred times the underlying, populated by tens of millions of individuals who are statistically certain to lose. There's no other market like it. The United States has a bigger options market in dollars and a far smaller one in participation. Korea had the wildest index options market on earth in 2011 and regulated it into a corner within a year. India's regulator is tightening too, with bigger lot sizes and fewer weekly expiries, and the number of active traders fell 18 percent last year. It's still the largest casino in the world where the house seats are open to anyone with a Mumbai office and a market-making licence.

So I don't think the right lesson for a hedge fund is "stay away." Every quant firm I'd want to own a piece of should have a desk in India and a compliance officer who has read the Jane Street order twice. The edge isn't a trick. The edge is that a country of 1.4 billion people has decided, for now, to pay a toll of about $10 billion a year to whoever is fast enough to collect it, and has posted the tariff in a government PDF. Toll roads are good businesses, and the ones with a published rate are the best ones.
