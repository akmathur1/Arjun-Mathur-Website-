Fifty thousand times a second a droplet of molten tin, 25 micrometres across, falls through a vacuum chamber and a carbon-dioxide laser hits it twice. The first pulse flattens it into a pancake. The second turns the pancake into a plasma at about 220,000 degrees Celsius, forty times the surface of the sun, and the tin ions, relaxing, give off light at 13.5 nanometres. That wavelength is the whole point and the whole problem. Nearly everything absorbs it, air and glass included, so no lens can focus it. Mirrors do the work, about ten of them, each a hundred alternating layers of molybdenum and silicon, each reflecting at most 68 percent. Ten reflections later, about one percent of the light that leaves the plasma reaches the wafer. Zeiss polishes those mirrors for two to three years apiece to a roughness measured in picometres, a fraction of an atom; scaled to the area of Germany, the tallest bump would stand under a millimetre.

One company on earth assembles this into a machine that works: ASML (ASML), in Veldhoven. A standard EUV scanner costs around $180 million. The High-NA version costs about $400 million and only about a dozen exist; Intel has run more than a million wafers through its machines, Samsung plans volume production on them in 2028 and TSMC in 2030. Last year ASML recognised revenue on 48 EUV systems and €32.7 billion of sales. On October 14 it reported a record quarter, €11.7 billion at a 57.5 percent gross margin, €4.1 billion of it EUV. In January it guided 2026 at €34 to €39 billion. It now says €44 to €45 billion, and €44 billion was the floor of the 2030 plan it laid out in November 2024: the bottom of its own decade target, four years early. Supply as planned for 2027 and 2028, the chief executive said, will not meet demand. Customers asking to reserve slots for 2029 and 2030 are being told to wait.

China has never been allowed an EUV machine. The Dutch do not license them, and since 2023 and 2024 the better deep-ultraviolet immersion tools have needed licences too, granted case by case. So Chinese fabs bought the older ones, more than $13 billion on roughly 180 NXT:1980i scanners in 2024 and 2025 by one count, and China was 41 percent of ASML's system sales in 2024, 33 percent last year and 23 percent in the quarter just reported. In Washington the MATCH Act, introduced in April, would end even that: all immersion lithography and the servicing of tools already installed, with the Netherlands given 150 days to copy the rule or watch the foreign direct product rule do it for them. It was folded into the Senate defence bill, and the Senate blocked that bill on July 14 in a fight over paying for the Iran war.

Now the price. The company whose supply is sold out for three years, whose nearest competitor does not exist, whose customers are begging for 2030, fell nearly two percent on October 14 and another two after hours because sales came in at €11.66 billion against a consensus of €11.72 billion. Half a percent. The shares have traded between $946 and $2,000 in twelve months, the range of a semiconductor-equipment cyclical, and ASML was one for decades, when fabs ordered scanners in waves and cancelled them in waves. The market still prices the quarter and not the queue. At 36 times next year's earnings it is not cheap, but its own January guidance was eight billion euros short, and the price increases on its critical tools only begin to show in 2027. If I were going to own one semiconductor company it would be the one that does not make semiconductors.

One percent of the light reaches the wafer. Every chip at the frontier is drawn with that one percent.
