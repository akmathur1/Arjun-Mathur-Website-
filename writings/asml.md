I've been trying to explain to friends why I find ASML more interesting than Nvidia, and it usually goes badly, so let me try it in writing.

Inside an EUV machine a droplet of molten tin about 25 micrometres across falls through a vacuum chamber fifty thousand times a second. A carbon-dioxide laser hits each droplet twice. The first pulse flattens it into a pancake, and the second turns the pancake into a plasma at something like 220,000 degrees Celsius, roughly forty times the surface of the sun. As the tin ions relax they give off light at 13.5 nanometres. That wavelength is the reason the machine exists and also the reason it's so hard to build, because almost everything absorbs it, air and glass included. You can't focus it with a lens. So the machine uses mirrors, about ten of them, each made of around a hundred alternating layers of molybdenum and silicon, and each reflecting at most 68 percent of what hits it. After ten bounces about one percent of the light that left the plasma reaches the wafer. Zeiss spends two to three years polishing each mirror to a roughness measured in picometres, a fraction of an atom. The comparison I've seen is that if you scaled one up to the size of Germany, the tallest bump on it would be under a millimetre.

Exactly one company turns all of that into a machine that works, ASML (ASML), in Veldhoven. A standard EUV scanner costs around $180 million. The High-NA version is about $400 million and only about a dozen exist so far. Intel has put more than a million wafers through its machines, Samsung plans volume production on them in 2028 and TSMC in 2030. Last year ASML recognized revenue on 48 EUV systems and did €32.7 billion of sales. On October 14 it reported a record quarter, €11.7 billion at a 57.5 percent gross margin, €4.1 billion of it EUV. Back in January it had guided 2026 at €34 to €39 billion. It now says €44 to €45 billion, and €44 billion was the bottom of the 2030 range it laid out in November 2024, so it has reached the floor of its own decade plan four years early. The CEO said on the call that supply as planned for 2027 and 2028 won't meet demand, and that customers asking to reserve slots for 2029 and 2030 are being told to wait.

China has never been allowed an EUV machine. The Dutch don't license them, and since 2023 and 2024 the better deep-ultraviolet immersion tools have needed licenses too, granted case by case. So Chinese fabs bought the older ones, more than $13 billion worth on roughly 180 NXT:1980i scanners across 2024 and 2025 by one count. China was 41 percent of ASML's system sales in 2024, 33 percent last year and 23 percent in the quarter just reported. There's a bill in Washington, the MATCH Act, introduced in April, that would end even that: all immersion lithography, plus the servicing of tools already installed, with the Netherlands given 150 days to copy the rule or have the foreign direct product rule do it for them. It got folded into the Senate defense bill, and the Senate blocked that bill on July 14 in a fight over paying for the Iran war. So it's stalled, for now.

Here's the part I can't get over. This company, with supply sold out for three years, no competitor to speak of, and customers begging for 2030, fell nearly two percent on October 14 and another two after hours because sales came in at €11.66 billion against a consensus of €11.72 billion. That's a miss of half a percent. The shares have traded between $946 and $2,000 in twelve months, which is the kind of range you'd expect from a semiconductor-equipment cyclical, and to be fair ASML was exactly that for decades, back when fabs ordered scanners in waves and canceled them in waves. I think the market is still pricing the quarter instead of the queue. At 36 times next year's earnings it isn't cheap, I'll grant that. But its own January guidance turned out to be eight billion euros too low, and the price increases on its most critical tools don't start showing up until 2027.

If I had to own one semiconductor company it would probably be the one that doesn't make semiconductors. I don't own it yet, mostly because I keep waiting for a cheaper entry that the order book says isn't coming.
