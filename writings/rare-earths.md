A kilogram of dysprosium oxide costs about $200 in China this month and somewhere between $2,500 and $5,500 outside it. Terbium oxide is $900 inside and $10,000 to $13,000 out. Same molecule, same purity, a factor of ten to twenty for crossing a border. That spread is the price of a Chinese export licence, and the licence regime that produces it has now been in force for eighteen months, through three summits and two suspensions, with no sign of being lifted.

The dates matter because people keep conflating them. On April 4, 2025, Beijing put seven heavy rare earths, dysprosium and terbium among them, under case-by-case licensing. On October 9 it went much further: five more elements, controls on separation equipment, and an extraterritorial rule that would have required a Chinese licence for any magnet made anywhere containing 0.1 percent by value of Chinese material. When Trump and Xi met in Busan on October 30, China suspended the October package for a year, and Washington suspended its own Entity List affiliates rule, cut the fentanyl tariff and paused port fees. The April controls were never part of the deal. They are still there. Yttrium exports to the United States fell from over 333 tonnes in the eight months before the restrictions to 17 in the eight months after. Magnet exports to America ran at 512 tonnes in August, down 13 percent on the year; Chinese customs showed shipments to the US falling another 29 percent in September. General licences introduced last December cover three Chinese producers and exclude any defence end-user. Beijing confirmed on September 28 that the broader truce runs to January 10, 2027, and its statement did not mention rare earths by name. The suspension that was supposed to lapse on November 10 is, as I write, formally unextended.

So the chokehold is real. The question is where it sits, and most people have the geology wrong. China mines about 60 percent of magnet rare earths. That is a large share, but Mountain Pass in California is one of the richest deposits on earth, Lynas (LYC) mines in Western Australia, and Brazil, Vietnam and Greenland have ore. Rare earths are not rare. What China has is 91 percent of separation and 94 percent of sintered magnet production, and separation is the step that matters, because the fifteen lanthanides are chemically near-identical: same outer electron configuration, ionic radii that differ by a few picometres down the row. You cannot smelt them apart. You pull them apart with thousands of stages of solvent extraction, each stage exploiting a partition coefficient barely above one, and China spent thirty years and a great deal of poisoned groundwater learning how to run those cascades at scale. The leverage is a process. Processes are harder to copy than mines and easier to defend than tariffs.

Washington worked this out and did something I did not expect from this administration: it bought the process. In July 2025 the Pentagon put $400 million into MP Materials (MP) preferred stock and became its largest shareholder, guaranteed a floor of $110 a kilogram for NdPr for ten years, lent $150 million for heavy separation at Mountain Pass, and agreed to buy every magnet from a second Texas plant, 10X, that broke ground in February and should take US capacity to about 10,000 tonnes a year on commissioning in 2028. Apple signed for $500 million of recycled magnets from the Fort Worth plant the following week. MP's second quarter showed $17.6 million of price-floor income on NdPr realised in the high nineties; the first heavy-separation circuit reached mechanical completion in May. If I were going to own this trade I would own MP and Lynas, which this year is the only producer of separated dysprosium and terbium of any size outside China, at eight tonnes a quarter.

Eight tonnes. The world uses something like 180,000 to 200,000 tonnes of rare earth magnets a year and the United States imported about 40,000 of them in 2024, more than 90 percent from China. Vacuumschmelze is shipping from South Carolina, Noveon from Texas, and the IEA puts the bill for a diversified supply chain at around $60 billion over a decade, which is less than Meta borrowed in two years. The money is not the constraint. The constraint is that a solvent-extraction cascade takes years to commission and a decade to run well, and no fact sheet can shorten that.

The truce expires on a date in January. The partition coefficients do not expire at all.
