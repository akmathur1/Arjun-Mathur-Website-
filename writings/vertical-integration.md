Sixty-three cents of every dollar Optum Rx booked in 2025 came from another part of UnitedHealth Group (UNH). The pharmacy benefit manager filled 1.66 billion prescriptions and took in $155 billion; its largest customer is the insurer that owns it. Optum Health employs or affiliates more than 90,000 physicians, about one in ten in the country. Optum Insight owns Change Healthcare, the clearinghouse that moves 15 billion transactions a year and touches one in three patient records. Optum Bank holds the health savings accounts. One company is the payer, the doctor, the pharmacist and the rail the money runs on, and it booked $447.6 billion of revenue in 2025, up 12 percent.

CVS Health owns Aetna and Caremark. Cigna owns Express Scripts. Those three PBMs processed 80 percent of the country's prescription claims last year, and Humana runs the same model smaller. The industry's word for this is integration; the pitch is that one owner holding every step takes the friction out. A market, like a heat engine, runs on a difference: insurer and provider are the hot and cold reservoirs, and the price is the work done between them. Put both in one hand and there is no gradient and no work, only heat, and the heat is $155 billion of internal billing nobody outside the building ever negotiated.

The market noticed late. UnitedHealth closed at a record $596 a share in November 2024, worth about $560 billion. In April 2025 it cut its forecast and lost 22 percent in a day; what the release called "heightened care activity" was people using the insurance they had paid for. In May the chief executive left and the guidance went with him. By December the company was worth about $300 billion; it is back near $370 billion today. The 2025 medical care ratio was 89.1 percent: 89 cents of each premium dollar went to care.

In July 2025 the company disclosed criminal and civil Justice Department investigations into its Medicare billing, widened by August to Optum Rx and to how it pays its own doctors. A Senate Judiciary staff report in January found it captured more diagnosis codes than any other Medicare Advantage insurer and called risk adjustment "a major profit centered strategy." The company disputes it. On denials the record is also the Senate's: UnitedHealthcare's prior-authorization denial rate for post-acute care went from 10.9 percent in 2020 to 22.7 percent in 2022. Across the exchange market, insurers denied 19 percent of in-network claims in 2024; fewer than one percent were appealed.

On December 4, 2024, Brian Thompson, who ran the insurance arm, was shot dead on a Manhattan sidewalk on his way to the company's investor day. That was murder, and nothing in a denial letter or a 10-K excuses it. His killer pleaded guilty to federal stalking charges in August and will be sentenced in December; the state murder case is stalled on a double-jeopardy motion. What should trouble the company more than the trial is the public reaction, which was not grief. It told you how many people had met the structure at its worst with nobody to send the anger to, because the insurer, the doctor and the pharmacist were the same voicemail.

Violence does not break that structure. It hardens it: a company under threat gets sympathy, a bigger legal budget and a licence to call every critic dangerous. What breaks it is a statute. Arkansas tried in April 2025, banning PBMs from owning pharmacies; a federal judge enjoined the law and the appeal sits at the Eighth Circuit. Congress passed PBM transparency in February, effective in 2028. Transparency is what a legislature passes when it has decided not to touch ownership. The FTC settled its insulin case with all three PBMs by June; no settlement changes who owns whom.

The denial, the diagnosis, the prescription and the cheque carry four signatures from one hand.
