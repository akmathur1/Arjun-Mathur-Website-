On Wednesday Aurora (AUR) told its shareholders it has built twenty driverless trucks. In February the target was two hundred by year end; that is now the end of February, revenue guidance has gone from a $15 million midpoint to $9 million, the quarter brought in $3 million against a $234 million net loss, and a company valued near $10 billion fell 7 percent after hours. Kodiak (KDK), down 87 percent since listing last year, signed its first binding highway contract: seventy-five trucks for a Fresno carrier, from 2028. Tesla's Semi reached volume production in September, seven years late, and is not autonomous.

Aurora's trucks have run half a million commercial miles with nobody aboard, including the thousand miles from Fort Worth to Phoenix no human may legally drive without stopping. A thousand trucks are promised for the end of 2027; one carrier has signed for five hundred, non-binding. The $250 million Aurora expects to burn this quarter is binding.

The 1,300 percent growth to 2036 I kept hearing is not Goldman's; it is a research shop's $1.3 billion last year becoming $17.7 billion. Goldman's April forecast is more useful: a $560 billion global market by 2035, cost parity with a human driver in the United States in 2028, and a cost per mile falling from $8.56 today to $2.03, against a driver at $2.55 now and $2.84 then. A truck that runs 80 cents a mile cheaper than a person and never hits an hours-of-service limit does not need to be clever. It needs to be allowed.

The forecast leaves something out. Once the driver is software, the truck is a commodity Volvo and Paccar build on their own lines, and the software is a per-mile licence sold by two or three vendors with no pricing power. Aurora and Kodiak both call themselves asset-light: the carrier buys the truck and rents the driver by the mile. Then who makes the money? Whoever has the loads. Matching freight to trucks across a continent is a demand problem, and one company has spent sixteen years on nothing else.

I own Uber (UBER), and the market has decided it is a ride-hailing company about to be eaten by robotaxis. It closed at $65.78 on Tuesday, a 52-week low, 35 percent below its high, fourteen times trailing earnings, with $10 billion of trailing free cash flow for the first time. Bookings grew 24 percent last quarter. Waymo left Uber's app in Phoenix in July and opens its own in Austin and Atlanta in 2028; it runs about 4,000 cars. Uber did 3.9 billion trips in a quarter, under half a percent of them autonomous. The disruption is real and small, and priced as large and certain.

Freight is the part nobody models. Uber Freight manages more than $20 billion of it; Aurora, Volvo's autonomous unit and Waabi have all hauled its loads between Dallas and Houston; Uber owns a piece of Aurora from selling it its self-driving unit in 2020 and backed Waabi's round. It builds no truck and writes no driver. In Rust I would call it a trait: the caller never learns which concrete type sits behind it, only that the method is implemented. The market is pricing the trait as one of the implementations.

The counter-argument is serious. A demand layer has power only while supply is fragmented. If Waymo ends up with most robotaxis and two firms with most trucks, they sell direct and Uber's take rate becomes a toll the owners refuse to pay. The founder of Uber Freight left last year to run operations at Waabi, which tells you where he thinks the power sits.

My bet is the other way, because every autonomy company is becoming a thin-margin seller of software by the mile, and none of them has a customer. A truck with no driver still needs someone to tell it where to go.
