On September 1 Nintendo raised the American price of the Switch 2 from $449.99 to $499.99, fifteen months after launch, with the console outselling the PlayStation 5 more than two to one. Companies do not usually do that in a console's second year. Nintendo (NTDOY) looked at memory prices driven up by the data-centre build, looked at tariffs, and decided the customer would pay. So far the customer has.

The numbers as of June 30: 23.68 million Switch 2 consoles sold since the June 2025 launch, 19.86 million of them in the first fiscal year against a launch-day forecast of 15 million. Sony's (SONY) PlayStation 5 shipped 1.6 million units in the June quarter. The Switch 2 shipped 3.82 million, and Nintendo described that quarter as down 34 percent, because the launch frenzy it was lapping was so large. The PS5 has 95.3 million consoles in the world after five and a half years. The Switch 2 is a quarter of the way there after one.

Then the software. Switch 2 owners have bought 58.17 million games, about two and a half a console. The original Switch, at 156.59 million units, ended up carrying ten games each over its life, 1.56 billion in total. Mario Kart World alone has sold 15.39 million copies. The Super Mario Galaxy Movie passed a billion dollars at the box office this spring, and Nintendo's income from licensing its characters more than doubled in the June quarter, to ¥34.8 billion. Sony owns a very good box. Nintendo owns a plumber that three generations recognise on sight.

The index committees file Nintendo under Communication Services, in a sub-industry called Interactive Home Entertainment, beside Netflix and Alphabet rather than beside the companies that make the television it plugs into. The classification is more honest than it looks. Nintendo is a media company that happens to manufacture its own distribution.

Here is how it wins, and it is the way it has won since the Game Boy. It refuses the spec war. Sony and Microsoft compete on one axis, rendering power, and on that axis Nintendo has been losing by choice since the Wii; the trick is that Nintendo is not on the axis. It sells a box good enough to run Mario, sells the box at a margin rather than as a loss leader, and then sells the software at a much fatter margin for seven or eight years to an installed base no other publisher can reach. Its customers are children. Children become parents, and parents buy the box they remember.

Now the price. The shares closed at ¥14,795 in August 2025 and trade near ¥8,500 today, down about 42 percent, for a market value around ¥9.8 trillion, roughly $63 billion. On trailing earnings that is 21 times. On forward earnings it is about 30, and the gap between those two numbers is the whole argument. Nintendo's own forecast for the year to March 2027 is 16.5 million consoles against 19.86 million, net sales down 11 percent to ¥2.05 trillion, profit down 27 percent to ¥310 billion, with about ¥100 billion of memory and tariff costs written into it. The market is paying thirty times a year the company itself says will be worse.

Is that right? Partly. Nintendo guides low; the 15 million became 19.86 million, and sell-through is running ahead of the original Switch at the same point in its life. But the memory squeeze is real, the price rise will cost units, and a $500 console is a different purchase from a $450 one in a household paying more for everything else. I would own it below ¥8,000, and not as a console cycle. I would own it as the only media company whose audience is replaced every seven years and arrives already knowing the characters.

Mario is forty-five years old. His customers are seven, and they always will be.
