A 65-inch television costs what the shelf tag says it costs until the moment someone carries it back through the door. Then it costs something else. Nothing happened to the panel. The pixels did not age in the eleven days it sat in my living room. But the store cannot put it back on the shelf as new, so a second sticker goes on the box, and the second sticker says open box, and open box means a discount, and the discount is often large.

So here is a small game I play. Buy the television. Return it inside the window, intact, receipt in hand. Come back a few days later and find the same unit on the clearance rack wearing the second sticker. Buy it again. Pay cash on at least one leg, preferably both, because I would rather the loyalty database not draw a line between the two transactions, and because a game the store would prefer you did not play is still a game you are allowed to play.

I am not writing this to teach anyone how to shave a few hundred dollars off a Hisense. I am writing it because it is the cleanest demonstration I know of a fact most people never fully absorb: price is not a property of the object. Price is a story the seller tells about the object, and the story can be rewritten by events that never touch the thing itself.

Physicists have a name for a quantity that depends only on the state of a system and not on its history. The television is a state function. Its price is not. Its price carries a path: who held it, for how long, whether a box was opened, whether a form was filled in. The retailer's accounting cannot see the panel. It can only see the path.

Markets do this at scale, every day, with better instruments. A bond gets cut one notch below investment grade and every fund whose mandate forbids junk must sell it that week, whether or not a single cash flow changed. A stock falls out of an index and the index funds dump it on rebalance day. A company reports one bad quarter and the word distressed goes on the box. Same cash flows, new sticker. Someone who is only permitted to own clean boxes has to sell to someone who is willing to look inside.

That second buyer is who I try to be. Not because open boxes are always bargains; they are not, and sometimes the panel really is cracked. But the discount for a damaged label is paid by people who are not allowed to check and collected by people who are.

The return desk is the most honest counter in the store. It is where the seller admits, in writing, that the first price was only ever a story.
