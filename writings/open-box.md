I bought a 65-inch television in June, kept it for eleven days, and took it back. There was nothing wrong with it. The pixels didn't age in my living room. But once something comes back through the return door the store can't sell it as new anymore, so a second sticker goes on the box that says open box, and open box means a discount, and the discount is often large.

So here's a small game I play. Buy the television. Return it inside the window, intact, receipt in hand. Go back a few days later and find the same unit on the clearance rack wearing the second sticker. Buy it again. I pay cash on at least one leg, preferably both, partly because I'd rather the loyalty database not draw a line between the two transactions, and partly because a game the store would rather you didn't play is still a game you're allowed to play.

I'm not writing this up to teach anyone how to save a few hundred dollars on a Hisense. I'm writing it because it's the cleanest demonstration I know of something most people never quite absorb, which is that price isn't a property of the object. Price is a story the seller tells about the object, and the story can get rewritten by events that never touch the thing itself.

Physicists have a name for a quantity that depends only on the state of a system and not on its history: a state function. The television is one of those. Its price isn't, because the price carries a path with it: who held it, for how long, whether a box got opened, whether a form got filled in. The retailer's accounting can't see the panel, only the path.

Markets do the same thing at scale, every day, with better instruments. A bond gets cut one notch below investment grade and every fund whose mandate forbids junk has to sell it that week, whether or not a single cash flow changed. When a stock drops out of an index, the index funds dump it on rebalance day. One bad quarter and the word distressed goes on the box. The cash flows are the same and the sticker is new, and someone who's only permitted to own clean boxes has to sell to someone who's willing to look inside.

That second buyer is who I try to be. Not because open boxes are always bargains. They aren't, and sometimes the panel really is cracked. But the discount for a damaged label is paid by people who aren't allowed to check and collected by people who are, and I'd rather be in the second group.

The return desk, I've decided, is the most honest counter in the store. It's the one place where the seller admits in writing that the first price was only ever a story. The TV, for what it's worth, works fine.
