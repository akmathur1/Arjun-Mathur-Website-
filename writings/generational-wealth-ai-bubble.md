Four companies will spend about $740 billion on data centers this year, up from roughly $140 billion in 2023, and they're no longer paying for all of it out of cash flow. Alphabet sold a hundred-year bond in February at 6.125 percent to buy servers it will depreciate in five or six years, then announced an $85 billion equity raise in June. Meta announced a $95 billion stock sale on October 26, and Microsoft came back to the bond market three days later for the first time since 2017 and took $25 billion. Three of the four now report negative free cash flow, and American investment-grade issuance passed $1.76 trillion in late October, a record with two months left in the year.

Everyone I know calls this the AI bubble, and I agree there's one. I think they're pointing at the wrong part of it.

Look at what actually went up. Through September 30 the S&P 500 semiconductor index was up 44 percent for the year and the hardware index 36 percent, while the four companies writing the checks were up 8 percent on average on 27 percent earnings growth. Samsung, Micron and SK Hynix all crossed a trillion dollars of market value in May, SK Hynix after a twelve-month gain of more than 1,000 percent, and a memory ETF that launched on April 2 had doubled by the end of that month. Then in mid-October a Chinese DRAM maker announced a server memory product, and SK Hynix lost 26 percent in five days, Samsung 22, Micron 11. That's how a commodity with a cycle behaves. The private side is further gone: AI companies took 82.7 percent of US venture dollars through the third quarter, the highest share PitchBook has ever recorded. Capital runs to the new thing, and the boring, high-quality businesses get left on the shelf.

The spenders are a different animal. The sell side models every dollar of the capex and almost none of the revenue it produces. Consensus has Meta's capital spending at 50 to 65 percent of revenue through 2029 while its revenue growth slows from 27 percent this year to 15. Both can't be true. Either the spending earns a return and revenue beats the estimates, or it doesn't and the budgets get cut, and free cash flow snaps back either way. A gigawatt of compute costs $40 to $50 billion to build with current chips and rents for $15 to $20 billion a year. At a 50 percent incremental margin that's a pre-tax return on capital of 15 to 25 percent, and the servers pay for themselves in under three years. AWS grew 39 percent in the third quarter on a $578 billion backlog, and Amazon guided to more than 40 percent growth in both 2027 and 2028. Azure grew 48 percent, Meta's revenue 26, and Alphabet's cloud revenue doubled. None of these stocks went up on the news. Alphabet had its worst day ever after reporting $40 billion of quarterly operating income and $9 billion of negative free cash flow.

The bears also have rates wrong. On September 16 the Fed raised the funds rate for the first time since 2023, to 3.75 to 4 percent, and the October earnings calls answered with bigger budgets for 2027. Demand for compute doesn't respond to a quarter point, because whoever gets to general intelligence first earns a return that doesn't fit in a discount model, and nobody in that race is going to be the one who blinked over 25 basis points. Higher rates will show up in the price of intelligence rather than in less of it.

So here are my rules. The first is a ten-year test. Would I own it if the market closed for a decade, or if something ugly hit the economy in year three? Not thriving, standing. Meta (META) passes: 3.6 billion people a day, a social graph that doesn't depreciate, and the cash flow to stay in the race from behind if it has to. Microsoft (MSFT) passes: 450 million people live inside its productivity suite, 30 million of them now pay for Copilot, and it sold for 19 times earnings at midyear because the market decided chatbots would eat Office. Amazon (AMZN) passes. Restaurant Brands (QSR) passes: 33,401 restaurants selling the cheapest hot calories in town, a demand that gets stronger in a recession, at 17 times earnings. Now try the test on SK Hynix or Micron. Ask what happens to high-bandwidth memory pricing when four customers, who are also designing their own chips, decide they've bought enough for a year. Most people who own them can't answer. They own a ticker that went up. The test isn't whether this is a good business. It's whether you know what kills it.

The second rule is don't get greedy, and know what greed feels like from the inside, because it doesn't feel like greed. What the people around you are experiencing is a number getting larger, which is a different thing from getting richer until they convert it, and almost nobody converts it. Pay the tax. Long-term federal capital gains top out at 23.8 percent and the bubble's discount has no cap. When a position has tripled and something better is on sale at 19 times earnings, selling the first to buy the second is the whole job, and the tax is the price of admission. Decide in advance what you'll sell and when, write it down, and move the proceeds into an account you treat as sealed, so future you can't talk present you out of the exit. Never selling is how the paper wealth of 2000 and 2021 turned back into paper.

The third rule is no leverage. None. No margin, no zero-day options, no perpetual futures, no loan against the portfolio to buy more portfolio. Every bubble has some mechanism by which a correct long-term view gets liquidated at the bottom, and the mechanism is always a margin call. India's retail traders lost about $10 billion in derivatives last year. Korean margin debt is above 34 trillion won against an index that has more than doubled since the start of last year, there are now leveraged single-stock ETFs on Samsung and SK Hynix, and the forced selling has already started. Some of those people might even be right about the thesis. Their balance sheet won't let them stay around to find out. The only money you can hold through a crash is money you can lose without getting a phone call.

The fourth rule is to buy what the rotation throws away, because a bubble is a sorting machine and right now it's sorting wrong. Uber (UBER) made new 52-week lows in the last week of October, under $66, with $10.4 billion of free cash flow over the past twelve months. It trades at 16 times earnings, the cheapest it has ever been, because the market thinks robotaxis will eat it. Uber expects about 700 driverless cars on its network by year-end and more than 2,000 next year, paid for with its partners' capital rather than its own, which is the whole point of being the aggregator. Visa (V) and Mastercard (MA) were marked down to 22 times earnings this spring on the theory that stablecoins and shopping agents would route around them. They take about 20 basis points of a transaction for fraud protection and dispute resolution, and an agent spending my money unsupervised needs both of those more, not less.

S&P Global (SPGI) fell more than a quarter in February after Anthropic shipped a desktop agent and the company guided below its own targets, which took the multiple from 25 to 19. More than 80 percent of its profit is Ratings, Indices and Platts, and every one of those AI bonds gets rated on the way out the door. Netflix (NFLX) sits at about half its June 2025 high after losing Warner Bros. Discovery to Paramount, collecting $2.8 billion for losing, and growing cash content spend 2 percent a year since 2021 while its operating margin went from 21 percent to 34.6. Brookfield (BN) is the one real AI winner here that's priced like a loser: 11 times earnings, down 20 percent this year on private-credit worries it mostly doesn't have, while it builds 10.5 gigawatts of power for Microsoft, runs a $100 billion AI infrastructure program with Nvidia, and on November 3 announced a gigawatt-plus data center at the Drax power station in England.

Then hold cash on top. Real cash, boring cash, earning whatever it earns. The reset, when it comes, will start in the private rounds and the rented-GPU layer rather than in the four spenders. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps went above 1,100 basis points on October 16, while the companies it rents to have little or no net debt and market caps large enough that a $95 billion stock sale is a 6 percent dilution. What won't survive is the layer that financed growth with debt it can't service when a tenant walks: the data-center shells, the GPU lessors, the clouds with one customer, the chipmakers who invest in the startups that buy their chips. Anthropic filed to list on October 26. The time to buy is after the first drawdown, not on the first day.

The AI bubble will make a small number of people generationally wealthy. I'd bet almost none of them will be the ones who bought the chips.
