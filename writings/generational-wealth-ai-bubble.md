Four companies will spend about $725 billion on data centers this year, up 77 percent on 2025, and for the first time most of that money isn't coming out of cash flow. AI-linked companies have issued roughly $140 billion of investment-grade bonds so far in 2026, about half of all investment-grade issuance in America. Alphabet sold a hundred-year bond in February to pay for chips it will depreciate in five or six. Meta's debt went from $36 billion to $84 billion in two and a half years. More than 80 percent of the S&P 500's gain this year has come from AI names, and if you strip them out the index is up about two percent.

I'm not saying this ends tomorrow. I'm saying it ends, because every capital cycle financed with other people's money has ended, and for someone my age the interesting question isn't how to call the top. It's how to come out the other side owning more of the world than you went in with. These are my four rules. They aren't sophisticated. In my experience the sophisticated people are the ones who get hurt.

The first rule is a ten-year test. Before I buy anything I ask whether the company would still be standing if the world shut down for ten years, or if something catastrophic hit the economy in year three. Not thriving, just standing. Coca-Cola (KO) passes. People drank the product through two world wars, a depression and a pandemic, and the company has paid a dividend every year since 1920. Restaurant Brands (QSR) passes: thirty thousand restaurants selling people the cheapest hot calories in their town, which is a demand that gets stronger in a crisis, not weaker. Meta (META) passes too, which surprises people. It owns the social graph of more than three billion humans, the graph doesn't depreciate, and whatever superintelligence turns out to be, the company has the cash flow to stay in that race from behind if it has to.

Now try the same test on SK Hynix, or Broadcom, or Marvell. They're fine companies. But ask ten people who own them what the company sells, to whom, and what happens to the price of that product when the buyer's capital budget gets cut in half. Most can't answer. They own a ticker that went up, and that's the only fact they know about it. The test isn't whether this is a good business. It's whether you understand what kills it, and whether it can be killed. High-bandwidth memory is a commodity with a cycle. A social graph isn't.

The second rule is don't get greedy, and know what greed feels like from the inside, because it doesn't feel like greed. What the people around you are experiencing is a number getting larger. That's a different thing from getting richer, and the number only stays theirs once they convert it, and almost nobody converts it. They call themselves long-term investors when the actual mechanism is fear of the capital gains tax, so they never sell, and then the market sells for them at a price they didn't choose. Pay the tax. Long-term federal capital gains top out at 23.8 percent. The bubble's discount has no cap. Decide in advance what you'll sell and when, write it down, and move the proceeds into a separate account you treat as sealed, so that future you can't talk present you out of the exit. If you want real structure there are legal forms of it: gifting appreciated shares, a charitable remainder trust, a donor-advised fund. What doesn't exist is a trick that makes a realized gain untaxed. Anyone selling you that trick is really selling you a reason never to sell, and never selling is how the paper wealth of 2000 and 2021 turned back into paper.

The third rule is no leverage. None. No margin, no options you don't fully understand, no loan against the portfolio to buy more portfolio. Cash in, shares out, that's it. Every bubble has some mechanism by which a correct long-term view gets liquidated at the bottom, and the mechanism is always a margin call. India's retail traders lost about $10 billion in derivatives last year. Korean margin debt is above 34 trillion won against an index that has more than doubled since the start of last year, and the forced selling there has already started. Some of those people might even be right about the thesis. Their balance sheet won't let them stay around to find out. The only money you can hold through a crash is money you can lose without getting a phone call.

The fourth rule is to buy what the bubble is making distressed, because a bubble is a sorting machine and right now it's sorting wrong. Capital is draining out of everything that isn't AI and into everything that is, and the exits are mispriced. Gold is about a quarter below its January record. Uber (UBER) sits near a 52-week low because the market decided robotaxis will eat it, while it throws off close to $10 billion of free cash a year. Netflix (NFLX) is down about 44 percent in twelve months over an advertising guidance cut. Brookfield (BN) owns ports, pipelines, transmission lines, the kind of assets that produce cash for fifty years no matter which model is state of the art. None of these are exciting. Unexciting, cash-positive businesses are where most wealth that outlives a cycle comes from.

Then hold cash on top. Real cash, boring cash, earning whatever it earns. When the correction comes, the companies that are real will still be real and the labs that matter will still matter. If Anthropic or OpenAI ever list, the time to buy is after the first drawdown, not on the first day. What won't survive is the layer that financed growth with debt it can't service when revenue misses: the data-center shells, the GPU lessors, the clouds with one customer. Over-financed companies die in every cycle, and not because the product was bad. Debt has a maturity date and hype doesn't.

The AI bubble will make a small number of people generationally wealthy. I'd bet almost none of them will be the ones who bought the most AI.
