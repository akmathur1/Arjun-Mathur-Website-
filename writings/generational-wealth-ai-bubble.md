Four companies will spend about $725 billion on data centers this year, up 77 percent on 2025, and for the first time most of it is not coming out of cash flow. AI-linked companies have issued roughly $140 billion of investment-grade bonds so far in 2026, about half of all investment-grade issuance in America. Alphabet sold a hundred-year bond in February to pay for chips it will depreciate in five or six. Meta's debt went from $36 billion to $84 billion in two and a half years. More than 80 percent of the S&P 500's gain this year came from AI names; strip them out and the index is up about two percent.

I am not saying it ends tomorrow. I am saying it ends, because every capital cycle financed with other people's money has ended, and the question for someone my age is not how to call the top. It is how to come out the far side of the bubble owning more of the world than you went in with. Here are my four rules. They are not sophisticated. The sophisticated people are the ones who get hurt.

One. The ten-year test. Before I buy anything I ask whether the company would still be standing if the world shut down for ten years, or if something catastrophic hit the economy in year three. Not thriving. Standing. Coca-Cola (KO) passes: people drank the product through two world wars, a depression and a pandemic, and the company has paid a dividend every year since 1920. Restaurant Brands (QSR) passes: thirty thousand restaurants selling humans the cheapest hot calories in their town, a demand that intensifies in a crisis rather than vanishing. Meta (META) passes, which surprises people. It owns the social graph of more than three billion humans, the graph does not depreciate, and whatever superintelligence turns out to be, the company has the cash flow to stay in that race from behind if it has to.

Now run the same test on SK Hynix, or Broadcom, or Marvell. Fine companies. But ask ten people who own them what the company sells, to whom, and what happens to the price of that product when the buyer's capital budget is cut in half. Most cannot answer. They own a ticker that went up, which is the only fact they know about it. The test is not "is this a good business." The test is "do you understand what kills it, and can it be killed." High-bandwidth memory is a commodity with a cycle. A social graph is not.

Two. Do not get greedy, and understand what greed looks like from the inside. What the people around you are experiencing is a number getting larger, which is a different thing from getting richer, and the number stays theirs only until they convert it. Almost no one converts it. They call themselves long-term investors when the actual mechanism is fear of the capital gains tax, so they never sell, and then the market sells for them at a price they did not choose. Pay the tax. Long-term federal capital gains top out at 23.8 percent; the bubble's discount is not capped. Decide in advance what you will sell and when, in writing, and move the proceeds into a separate account you treat as sealed, so that future you cannot talk present you out of the exit. If you want real structure there are legal forms of it: gifting appreciated shares, a charitable remainder trust, a donor-advised fund. What does not exist is a trick that makes a realized gain untaxed. Anyone selling you that trick is selling you a reason never to sell, and never selling is how the paper wealth of 2000 and 2021 turned back into paper.

Three. No leverage. None. No margin, no options you do not fully understand, no loan against the portfolio to buy more portfolio. Cash in, shares out, the end. Every bubble has a mechanism by which a correct long-term view gets liquidated at the bottom, and the mechanism is always a margin call. India's retail traders lost about $10 billion in derivatives last year. Korean margin debt is above 34 trillion won against an index that has more than doubled since the start of last year, and the forced selling has already started. These are people whose thesis might even be right and whose balance sheet will not let them stay to find out. Only money you can lose without receiving a phone call is money you can hold through a crash.

Four. Buy what the bubble is making distressed, because the bubble is a sorting machine and it is sorting wrong. Capital is draining out of everything that is not AI and into everything that is, and the exits are mispriced. Gold is about a quarter below its January record. Uber (UBER) sits near a 52-week low because the market decided robotaxis will eat it, while it throws off close to $10 billion of free cash a year. Netflix (NFLX) is down about 44 percent in twelve months on an advertising guidance cut. Brookfield (BN) owns ports, pipelines, transmission lines and the kind of assets that produce cash for fifty years regardless of which model is state of the art. These are unsexy, cash-positive businesses, and unsexy cash-positive businesses are the whole root of wealth that outlives a cycle.

Then hold cash on top. Real cash, boring cash, earning whatever it earns. When the correction comes the companies that are real will still be real, and the labs that matter will still matter; if Anthropic or OpenAI ever list, the time to buy is after the first drawdown and not on the first day. What will not survive is the layer that financed growth with debt it cannot service when revenue misses: the data-center shells, the GPU lessors, the clouds with one customer. Over-financed companies die in every cycle, not because the product was bad, but because debt has a maturity date and hype does not.

The AI bubble will make a small number of people generationally wealthy. Almost none of them will be the ones who bought the most AI.
