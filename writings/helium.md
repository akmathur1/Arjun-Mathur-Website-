In March about two hundred cryogenic containers of liquid helium were stranded at Ras Laffan in Qatar. Each held roughly a million dollars of gas four degrees above absolute zero, and each was losing its load to boil-off within five or six weeks whether or not a ship came. The ships didn't come. The Strait of Hormuz has been closed or contested since the United States and Israel struck Iran on February 28, and it carries about a fifth of the world's LNG and about a third of its helium.

The helium is the part nobody planned for. Qatar's North Field gas is 0.04 percent helium, nearly every atom of it an alpha particle shed by uranium or thorium in the rock below, and QatarEnergy strips it out as a by-product of liquefaction, a little over two billion cubic feet a year, sold to Air Liquide, Linde and Iwatani. Iranian drones shut Ras Laffan on March 2. Missiles on March 18 and 19 wrecked two LNG trains, which is 17 percent of Qatar's liquefaction and 14 percent of its helium, and the chief executive says rebuilding will take about three years. By August the helium plants were at a quarter of capacity. Force majeure on Qatari cargoes has just been extended to the end of December, the strait is moving maybe 60 percent of normal crude under a US blockade of Iranian ports, and at the end of October mediators were in Tehran.

So the price did what prices do when a third of supply disappears and there's no substitute. Spot helium had doubled by the third week of March, with Europe near $450 per thousand cubic feet. By October a former Air Liquide executive was putting the spot move at a tripling, and long-term contracts, which are about 98 percent of the market and slow to reprice, are reported up 40 to 60 percent. American exports are up 70 percent since the war began, most of it going to chipmakers in Korea, Taiwan and Singapore, because Exxon's LaBarge field in Wyoming is about a fifth of world supply and the United States, at 43 percent, is the only producer that can flex. Russia, the third producer, put helium under export permits in April. South Korea bought 65 percent of its helium from Qatar last year, and by June some Korean and Taiwanese fabs were getting half their normal volume. On October 23 the Department of Energy added helium to its critical materials list, and the first industry it named was semiconductors.

Why helium specifically, and not some other gas? It's the only substance still liquid at 4.2 kelvin. The niobium-titanium magnets in an MRI scanner or a fusion experiment only superconduct below about ten kelvin, so a 1.5-tesla scanner sits in 1,500 liters of the stuff and the periodic table offers nothing else. In a fab the atom's lightness is the point. Blown across the back of a wafer in the etch chamber, it carries heat to the chuck faster than any heavier gas, and it finds leaks in vacuum systems because nothing smaller exists to slip through them. Semiconductors take about a fifth of the world's helium, roughly half the tools in a fab touch it, and a wafer that doesn't get etched today is a GPU that doesn't ship next quarter.

What I think the market has wrong is this. It prices the war in Brent, which was above $117 in late October, and in Qatar's LNG, which is why European gas is above €95. It doesn't price the inputs that are too small to carry a futures contract and too essential to do without, and helium is one of several. I own Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, and all four of their capital plans assume the wafers get etched. A reopened strait is the cheapest upgrade to those plans on offer, and I haven't seen an analyst model with that line in it.

One more thing about helium, which I find a bit sad. Once it's released it never comes back, because it's too light for the planet to hold onto. Qatar's has been leaving, a container at a time, since March.
