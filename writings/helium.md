In March about two hundred cryogenic containers of liquid helium were stranded at Ras Laffan in Qatar, each holding roughly a million dollars of gas four degrees above absolute zero, each losing its load to boil-off within five or six weeks whether or not a ship came. The ships did not come. The Strait of Hormuz has been closed or contested since the United States and Israel struck Iran on February 28, and it carries about a fifth of the world's LNG and about a third of its helium.

The helium is the part nobody planned for. Qatar's North Field gas is 0.04 percent helium, nearly every atom an alpha particle shed by uranium or thorium in the rock below, and QatarEnergy strips it out as a by-product of liquefaction, a little over two billion cubic feet a year, sold to Air Liquide, Linde and Iwatani. Iranian drones shut Ras Laffan on March 2. Missiles on March 18 and 19 wrecked two LNG trains, 17 percent of Qatar's liquefaction and 14 percent of its helium, which the chief executive says will take about three years to rebuild. By August the helium plants were at a quarter of capacity. Force majeure on Qatari cargoes has just been extended to the end of December, the strait is moving perhaps 60 percent of normal crude under a US blockade of Iranian ports, and mediators were in Tehran this week.

So the price did what prices do when a third of supply vanishes and no substitute exists. Spot helium had doubled by the third week of March, with Europe near $450 per thousand cubic feet; by October a former Air Liquide executive put the spot move at a tripling, and long-term contracts, about 98 percent of the market and slow to reprice, are reported up 40 to 60 percent. American exports are up 70 percent since the war began, most of it to chipmakers in Korea, Taiwan and Singapore, because Exxon's LaBarge field in Wyoming is about a fifth of world supply and the United States, at 43 percent, is the only producer that can flex; Russia, the third, put helium under export permits in April. South Korea bought 65 percent of its helium from Qatar last year, and by June some Korean and Taiwanese fabs were getting half their normal volume. On October 23 the Department of Energy added helium to its critical materials list and named semiconductors first.

Why helium and not something else. It is the only substance still liquid at 4.2 kelvin, and the niobium-titanium magnets in an MRI scanner or a fusion experiment superconduct only below about ten, so a 1.5-tesla scanner sits in 1,500 litres of it and the periodic table offers no other coolant. In a fab the atom's lightness is the point: blown across the back of a wafer in the etch chamber, it carries heat to the chuck faster than any heavier gas, and it finds leaks in vacuum systems because nothing smaller exists to slip through them. Semiconductors take a fifth of the world's helium, roughly half the tools in a fab touch it, and a wafer not etched today is a GPU not shipped next quarter.

Here is what I think the market has wrong. It prices this war in Brent, above $117 last week, and in Qatar's LNG, which is why European gas is above €95. It does not price the inputs too small to carry a futures contract and too essential to do without, and helium is one of several. I own Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, and their capital plans assume the wafers get etched. A reopened strait is the cheapest upgrade to those plans on offer, and no analyst model carries the line.

Released helium never comes back; it is too light for the planet to keep. Qatar's has been leaving, a container at a time, since March.
